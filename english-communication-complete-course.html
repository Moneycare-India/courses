Module 1
What Good Communication Is

The simple definition

Communication is when an idea moves from your head into someone else's head accurately. That's it. Everything else — grammar, vocabulary, accent — is in service of this one goal.

The four qualities of good communication

QualityWhat it means
ClearThe listener understood what you meant — not what you said
ConciseYou used the fewest words needed
CorrectFacts are right; grammar doesn't distract
ConsiderateYou thought about the listener's perspective

Notice "perfect grammar" isn't on this list. A grammatically broken sentence that's understood beats a grammatically perfect one that confuses.

The myth: "I need perfect English"

You don't. You need functional, confident, clear English. Many of the most successful people in international tech speak with strong accents and make grammar mistakes daily. What they have:

  • Confidence to speak even when imperfect.
  • Clarity about what they want to say.
  • Comfort with the rhythm and flow of English.
  • Awareness of how they come across.

The real goal of this course

To help you communicate ideas so the other person feels you understood them and they understood you. Not to make you sound American or British. Not to eliminate your accent. Not to teach you advanced literature vocabulary. Just to make every conversation work.

The 80/20 rule

20% of the language carries 80% of the meaning. Master:

  • Common 1000–2000 words.
  • Basic tenses (present, past, future).
  • Articles, prepositions, modal verbs.
  • Common phrases for greeting, asking, agreeing, disagreeing.
  • Pronunciation patterns that affect meaning.

You'll get 80% of communication ability with 20% of the work. The remaining 80% of effort is for the last 20% of polish.

The biggest secret

Listeners want you to succeed. When you speak with effort and clarity, people lean in to help you finish your point. Almost no one is judging your grammar. They're trying to understand your idea. Treat them as allies, not judges.

Module 2
English in the Tech Workplace

Why English specifically matters

English is the working language of global tech. Whatever your native language:

  • Your stack overflow searches are in English.
  • Documentation, error messages, libraries — English.
  • International clients, vendors, conferences — English.
  • Job interviews at multinational companies — English.
  • Career growth in product / leadership roles — English.

Your engineering skills get you the interview. Your communication skills get you the offer. Your ongoing communication skills get you promoted.

What "tech English" actually is

It's not Shakespeare. It's a relatively narrow subset of English with:

  • Technical vocabulary — already familiar from your work.
  • Direct, concise language — get to the point fast.
  • Plain phrasing — fewer idioms, shorter sentences.
  • Specific patterns — stand-up updates, status reports, code reviews, design discussions.

Good news: this means you don't need a vast literary vocabulary. You need a focused set of practical patterns.

Where communication makes or breaks careers

The communication ladder mirrors the career ladder

Junior developers code. Senior developers explain why their approach is right. Tech leads convince teams. Architects convince organizations. Each step requires more communication, not more code.

The good news

Tech English is learnable in months, not years. The vocabulary is bounded. The patterns are repeatable. The audience is empathetic — most engineers are non-native English speakers. Your peers want you to succeed.

Module 3
Common Challenges

The challenges most non-native speakers face

1. Confidence gap

You know more than you can express. You think in your native language, then translate, which slows you down. The translation lag makes you feel slow even when your thinking is fast.

Fix: stop translating. Use simpler English directly. Build muscle memory through speaking practice.

2. Pronunciation anxiety

You worry that your accent will be misunderstood or judged. So you speak less. So you practice less. So your accent doesn't improve.

Fix: accept your accent will never disappear and focus on clarity instead — stress, rhythm, intonation. People understand many accents; they don't understand mumbling.

3. Grammar paralysis

You stop mid-sentence to find the right tense, the right article. The pause kills the flow.

Fix: finish the sentence even if grammatically wrong. Speed matters more than correctness in spoken English.

4. Vocabulary gap

You can't think of the precise word. You substitute a wrong one or freeze.

Fix: learn paraphrasing — describe the concept when the word escapes you. "The thing that connects to..." beats silence.

5. Listening overwhelm

Native speakers talk too fast, with idioms and connected speech. You miss 30% and pretend you understood.

Fix: ask people to slow down or repeat. "Could you say that again?" is professional, not weak.

6. Cultural mismatch

Your way of disagreeing, requesting, or refusing may sound rude, weak, or confusing to international colleagues. Not a language issue — a cultural one.

Fix: learn the cultural patterns of the people you work with. Modules 36, 66, 67.

7. Indian-English habits the international audience misreads

"Kindly do the needful." "Please revert." "Out of station." These are 100% valid Indian English. International colleagues may find them confusing or overly formal.

Fix: for international audiences, swap to neutral international English. Module 67 has the specific list.

The compounding problem

These challenges feed each other. Low confidence → speak less → don't improve → confidence stays low. The escape: speak anyway. Bad English used confidently improves faster than perfect English used silently.

The fluency illusion

Native speakers don't actually have perfect grammar. They make mistakes constantly — repeat words, restart sentences, use wrong tenses. They're confident about it. Confident imperfect speech is what fluency looks like.

Module 4
Verbal, Non-verbal & Written

Three modes — different rules each

Verbal (face-to-face / video / phone)

  • Real-time. No edits. Errors fly past unnoticed.
  • Tone, pace, and volume carry meaning.
  • Tolerance for grammar mistakes is HIGH.
  • Silence is a signal — listener can't see you "thinking."
  • Interruptions, overlap are normal.

Non-verbal

Body language, facial expressions, gestures. Studies vary, but a good chunk of in-person communication impact is non-verbal:

  • Eye contact signals attention and confidence.
  • Nodding shows understanding.
  • Posture (open vs closed) signals openness.
  • Smile reduces tension.
  • Hand gestures aid emphasis (don't overuse).

On video calls: most non-verbal cues are weakened — only your face is visible. Voice tone has to do more work.

Written (email / Slack / docs / PRs)

  • Slow but precise. Every word can be edited.
  • Tolerance for grammar mistakes is LOWER.
  • No tone, pace, body language — emoji and word choice carry it all.
  • Permanent — what you wrote can be quoted forever.
  • Multiple readers possible (forwards, screenshots).

The big trap: tone in writing

"Please send the report."

In speech: with a smile, a friendly request. In writing: depending on the day, the reader hears you as polite, neutral, demanding, or aggressive. Written tone is a problem you must consciously solve.

How to inject warmth in writing

Match the medium to the message

The "phone call vs email" question for tech work

Modern engineering culture leans heavily toward async written communication. But:

  • If 3+ messages don't resolve it → switch to a call.
  • If emotions are involved → switch to a call.
  • If misunderstanding seems likely → switch to a call.
  • After the call → summarize in writing.

What this means in practice for international teams

Working with American / European teams (most common in tech)

  • They expect direct opinions. "I think" not "I am thinking maybe possibly that."
  • They expect concrete pushback. Silence = agreement to them.
  • They use first names regardless of seniority.
  • They appreciate brevity.
  • They value being on time, ending meetings on time.

The "yes" trap

In many Asian cultures including India, "yes" can mean "I'm listening" or "I respect what you said" — not "I agree" or "I will do it." International colleagues hear "yes" as agreement and feel betrayed when expectations aren't met.

Fix: use specific acknowledgments:

  • "Got it" — I heard you.
  • "That makes sense" — I understand the logic.
  • "I agree" — I agree.
  • "I'll do that by [date]" — commitment with timing.
  • "Let me check and get back to you" — I'm not yet committing.

Hierarchical respect doesn't mean silence

Some Indian engineers feel disrespectful disagreeing with senior people in meetings. International seniors usually want the disagreement — they consider silence as either agreement or lack of opinion. Both are bad.

Phrases for respectful disagreement covered in Module 33 and 49.

The high-context vs low-context insight

Indian English often relies on shared context: relationships, history, implied meaning. American English is low-context: spell things out explicitly. When working internationally, write more than feels necessary. State the obvious. Repeat the conclusion.

Module 6
The English Sound System

Why pronunciation isn't about spelling

English has roughly 26 letters but ~44 sounds. Letters and sounds don't match cleanly. read rhymes with red in past tense and seed in present. tough, through, thought all spell ough differently.

The fix: stop trusting spelling. Trust your ears. Listen and imitate.

The 44 sounds (phonemes)

Practice approach

  1. Pick one sound at a time.
  2. Find minimal pairs (ship/sheep, vine/wine, thin/tin).
  3. Listen, imitate, record yourself, compare.
  4. Don't try to fix everything at once.

  • Cambridge Dictionary — every word has audio for both UK and US pronunciation.

Module 7
Indian-English Pronunciation Pitfalls

The reality

Indian English is a complete, valid variety of English with ~125+ million speakers. It has its own valid features. But for international corporate communication — especially with American or British colleagues — some patterns reduce intelligibility.

This module is not "Indian English is wrong." It's: "if you want to be understood by international audiences, here are the specific things to adjust."

Top intelligibility issues, in priority order

1. v / w confusion

Many Indian languages have one sound that's between v and w. English has both, distinct.

  • v — top teeth touch lower lip. Vibrate. Like saying "f" but with voice.
  • w — round your lips like saying "oo," then move into the next sound.

Practice pairs: vine/wine, vest/west, vary/wary, vow/wow, vile/while.

2. th sounds

Most Indian languages have no /θ/ or /ð/. Speakers default to t or d.

  • /θ/ (think, three, thanks) — tongue between teeth, blow air. No voice.
  • /ð/ (this, that, mother) — same tongue position, but with voice.

Practice: thin/tin, three/tree, thanks, thought, mother, brother, the, this, that.

3. Word stress on the wrong syllable

This is THE biggest intelligibility issue. English meaning depends heavily on which syllable is stressed.

WordIndian-English stressInternational stress
developmentde-VEL-op-mentde-VEL-op-ment ✓ (often correct)
categorykuh-tay-GO-reeCAT-uh-go-ree

When in doubt, stress the second-to-last syllable for nouns, but check Cambridge Dictionary for important work words.

4. Adding extra vowels (epenthesis)

Indian languages don't allow consonant clusters easily. So speakers insert vowels between them.

WordCommon Indian-EnglishInternational
school"is-cool" or "su-cool""skool" — no leading vowel
star"is-tar""star"

Practice: pronounce sk/sp/st/str/spr at the start without any leading vowel.

5. p, t, k aspiration

English aspirates these at the start of words — a small puff of air. Hindi has both aspirated and unaspirated. Sometimes Indian English uses unaspirated where English wants aspirated.

Test: hold a tissue near your mouth and say "pin." It should flutter slightly. If it doesn't, you're not aspirating enough.

6. Final consonants devoiced

"Bag" sounds like "back," "had" sounds like "hat." Voicing the final consonant matters.

7. Speed

Many Indian-English speakers speak too fast. International audiences need extra time to process accents. Slowing down 20% increases your intelligibility 50%.

8. Monotone delivery

English uses pitch (intonation) to mark questions, emphasis, emotion. Indian English is often flatter. Module 8 covers this.

The triage approach

Don't try to fix everything. Pick the top 1–2 issues that cause your specific listeners to misunderstand. Often:

  1. Slow down.
  2. Stress the right syllable on important words.
  3. Pronounce v and w distinctly.
  4. Add aspiration to initial p/t/k.

These four alone close most of the gap.

Module 8
Stress, Rhythm & Intonation

The hidden rules of English

English isn't pronounced flat. It has a musical pattern — stressed syllables are louder, longer, higher; unstressed ones reduce. Get this right and you sound natural even with imperfect grammar. Get it wrong and people miss your meaning.

Word stress — which syllable is loud

The 3 patterns most words follow

PatternExamples
Stress on 1st syllable (most common for 2-syllable nouns/adjectives)TA-ble, MES-sage, COM-fort, PRO-blem, IM-port (noun)
Stress on 2nd syllable (most 2-syllable verbs)be-GIN, de-CIDE, im-PORT (verb), per-MIT (verb)
Stress on 2nd-to-last syllable (3+ syllable words ending in -tion, -sion, -ity, -ic, -ical)cre-A-tion, ad-MI-ssion, a-BIL-i-ty, e-CO-no-mic, his-TO-ri-cal

The noun-verb shift

Same spelling, different stress = different word.

  • RE-cord (noun) vs re-CORD (verb): "I broke the RE-cord." "Please re-CORD this meeting."
  • PRO-duce (noun, vegetables) vs pro-DUCE (verb): "We pro-DUCE PRO-duce."
  • CON-tract (noun) vs con-TRACT (verb).
  • EX-port (noun) vs ex-PORT (verb).

Sentence stress — content vs function words

Within a sentence, English speakers stress content words (the meaning carriers):

  • Stressed: nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question words.
  • Unstressed (reduced to schwa): articles (a, the), prepositions (to, of, for), pronouns (he, them), auxiliaries (was, have, will, can), conjunctions (and, but).

Example: "I'm going to the meeting at three"

Stressed: going meeting three. Other words reduced. Total length is roughly the same as a sentence with fewer words but those same content words.

Rhythm — the stress-timed pattern

English is stress-timed: stressed syllables come at roughly equal intervals. Unstressed syllables get squeezed between them.

Many Indian languages are syllable-timed: every syllable gets equal time. Speaking English with syllable-timed rhythm makes everything sound monotonous and "fast" to native ears even when you're going slowly.

Practice: tap a steady beat. Say content words on the beats; squeeze function words in between.

Intonation — pitch movement

The four patterns

PatternUseExample
Falling ↘Statements, wh-questions, commands"I'm going home." "What's your name?"
Rising ↗Yes/no questions, uncertainty"Are you ready?" "Really?"
Fall-rise ↘↗Hesitation, polite disagreement, lists (not last item)"Well... yes, but..." "I bought apples ↗, oranges ↗, and bananas ↘."
Rise-fall ↗↘Surprise, strong emphasis"Wow!" "Really!"

Question intonation common bug

Many Indian-English speakers say yes/no questions with FALLING intonation, which sounds like a statement to international audiences. Result: people don't realize you asked a question.

  • "You finished the report." (falling — sounds like statement)
  • "You finished the report?" (rising — clearly a question)

Emphasis — stress for meaning

Same sentence, different stress, different meaning:

  • I didn't say he stole the money. (Someone else said it.)
  • I didn't say he stole the money. (I implied it.)
  • I didn't say he stole the money. (Someone else stole it.)
  • I didn't say he stole the money. (He borrowed it.)
  • I didn't say he stole the money. (He stole something else.)

This is a uniquely English feature. Use it to clarify meaning without rephrasing.

Practice exercise

Pick a 30-second clip from any business podcast (Lex Fridman, Tim Ferriss, How I Built This). Transcribe it. Mark the stressed words. Listen and shadow — speak along, copying the rhythm and intonation exactly. 5 minutes a day for 30 days transforms this.

Module 9
Connected Speech

Why "spoken English doesn't sound like written English"

Native English speakers don't pronounce every word separately. Words connect, blend, and reduce. This is why you can read English fluently but understand spoken English poorly — you've been listening for separate words and they're not there.

The four main patterns

1. Linking — consonant + vowel

When a word ends in a consonant and the next starts with a vowel, they connect.

  • "turn it on" → "tur-ni-ton"
  • "pick it up" → "pi-ki-tup"
  • "check in" → "che-kin"

2. Reduction — function words become schwa

WrittenSpoken
going to"gonna"
want to"wanna"
have to"hafta"
has to"hasta"
got to"gotta"
let me"lemme"
give me"gimme"
What are you doing?"Whaddaya doin'?"
How are you?"Howyadoin?" / "How are ya?"

You don't need to speak with all these reductions. But you must recognize them when listening.

3. Assimilation — sounds change to match neighbors

  • "good boy" → often "goob boy" (d → b before b)
  • "green park" → often "greem park" (n → m before p)
  • "Did you?" → often "didja"
  • "Won't you?" → often "wonchu"

4. Elision — sounds disappear

  • "next day" → "nex' day" (t dropped)
  • "comfortable" → "comf'table"
  • "vegetables" → "vej'tables"
  • "library" → often "libr'ry"
  • "family" → often "fam'ly"

The takeaway for listeners

You don't hear the words you expect because the sounds blur together. Train your ear:

  • Watch English content with English subtitles. See the words while hearing the blur.
  • Slow down YouTube videos to 0.75x to hear the connections.
  • Shadow native speakers — repeat what they say in the same rhythm.

The takeaway for speakers

You can speak more carefully than natives — but don't go too slow with rigid word boundaries. Slight linking ("pi-ki-tup") sounds natural. Even small amounts of natural blur make you sound more fluent.

Module 10
Accent Reduction Strategy

Should you "reduce" your accent?

Honest answer: it depends on your goals.

GoalApproach
Be understood internationallyFocus on clarity: stress, rhythm, key sounds. Accent stays.
Sound American/BritishLong-term project (years). Possible but expensive.
Reduce specific issues that cause misunderstandingTargeted practice on 2–3 sounds. Months.
Build confidenceSpeak more, not less. Accept your accent.

Most working professionals should aim for "clearly intelligible Indian English" — not "American English." The world is full of Indian-accented engineers running successful companies. Accent isn't the problem; clarity is.

Self-assessment

  1. Record yourself reading a 1-minute paragraph (any technical document).
  2. Listen back. Note where YOU find your speech unclear.
  3. Ask 2 international colleagues to listen and tell you which words they had to guess.
  4. Their list = your priority list.

Practice methods that work

Shadowing (most effective)

Pick a 30-second native English clip. Listen 3 times. Then play it and speak ALONG with the speaker — match their rhythm, pace, intonation, pronunciation as closely as you can. Don't lag behind; speak simultaneously.

Why it works: forces you to use English's rhythm, not your native rhythm. Trains stress and intonation simultaneously with vocabulary.

Minimal pairs drill

Pick a sound you confuse (v/w, t/th). Find pair lists online. Read aloud, recording yourself. Compare to native pronunciation.

Read aloud daily

10 minutes/day reading any English text aloud. Builds mouth muscles, smooths transitions, and gradually internalizes patterns.

Watch with English subtitles

Trains the ear to map what you hear to what's written. Keep it on for everything for 6 months.

Talk to yourself in English

Narrate your day, your code, your thoughts in English when alone. Removes social pressure; builds fluency.

Tools (in priority order)

  1. YouGlish — free. Type any word, hear thousands of native speakers say it in real videos.
  2. ELSA Speak — paid app, AI feedback specifically tuned for Indian-English speakers.
  3. BBC Learning English / VOA Learning English — free, structured.
  4. Cambridge Dictionary — every word has UK + US audio.
  5. Conversation partner — italki, Cambly, language exchange. 1-on-1 practice with a tutor for $10–25/hour.

What progress looks like

Time investedRealistic outcome
1 month, 15 min/dayAwareness of your patterns; 1–2 sounds improving
3 months, 30 min/dayNoticeable clarity improvement; rhythm starts to feel natural
6 months, daily practicePeople comment that your English has improved
1 year, 1 hour/daySignificant accent reduction if that's the goal; or significant clarity boost if not

The honest truth

You'll have an accent for life. So will every other non-native speaker. Henry Kissinger had a thick German accent his whole life and was Secretary of State. Arnold Schwarzenegger became a movie star. Sundar Pichai runs Google with an Indian accent. Accent doesn't limit careers; communication does.

Spend your effort on being clear, structured, and confident. The accent will follow naturally when you speak more.

Module 11
Tenses in Spoken English

The good news

English has 12 tenses on paper. Spoken English uses about 4 heavily. Master those and you cover 90% of conversations.

The 4 tenses you actually need

1. Present Simple — habits, facts, schedules

I work at EasyAudit.
The system runs on .NET 8.
The deployment happens every Friday.
She doesn't like meetings before 10 AM.

2. Present Continuous — happening now or around now

I'm working on the login bug right now.
We're refactoring the auth module this sprint.
She's not joining the call today.
Are you waiting for the deploy to finish?

3. Past Simple — completed past actions

I deployed it yesterday.
We finished the migration last week.
The build broke at 3 AM.
Did you get my email?

4. Will / going to — future

I'll send it after lunch.
We're going to refactor next sprint.
The release will be on Friday.
I won't be in tomorrow.

The 2 you'll meet often

Present Perfect — past with present relevance

I have finished the task. (and it's done now)
We've deployed twice this week.
Have you reviewed the PR yet?
She hasn't joined the team yet.

The trickiest tense for many Indian English speakers because Hindi and other Indian languages don't have a direct equivalent. Common confusion with past simple:

Why modals matter

Modals (can, could, would, should, may, might, must, will) are how English speakers express politeness, possibility, obligation, and uncertainty. They're the soft layer that turns commands into requests.

The basic meanings

The politeness ladder for requests

Same request, increasing politeness:

  1. "Send me the report." (Direct command — can sound rude)
  2. "Please send me the report."
  3. "Can you send me the report?"
  4. "Could you send me the report?"
  5. "Could you possibly send me the report?"
  6. "Would you mind sending me the report?"
  7. "I was wondering if you could send me the report."

For corporate communication with people you don't know well, default to could or would. They sound polite without being overly formal.

Hedging — softening with modals

Don't say "this is wrong." Say:

  • "This might be off."
  • "There could be an issue here."
  • "This doesn't seem right to me."
  • "I'm not sure about this part."

Modals signal "I'm being collaborative, not aggressive" — important in code reviews, design feedback, disagreement.

  • shall — rare in modern American English. UK uses it for offers/suggestions ("Shall we?"). Avoid in international corporate.
  • should — recommendation, expectation. "You should restart it."
  • must — strong, formal. Use rarely. "You must comply with security policy."
  • have to — same as must but more conversational.

Module 16
Building Vocabulary Efficiently

The myth of "more vocabulary = better English"

An average native speaker uses ~3,000 words in daily conversation. Knowing 5,000 well beats knowing 50,000 vaguely. Quality over quantity.

The three vocabulary tiers

TierSizePurpose
Active vocabulary~2000 wordsWords you USE in speaking and writing
Passive vocabulary~10000+ wordsWords you UNDERSTAND when reading/listening
Recognition onlyUnlimitedWords you sort-of know in context

The asymmetry: you understand far more than you can produce. That's normal. Your goal isn't equal — it's to grow active vocabulary in areas you actually need.

Where to find words you actually need

  1. Your work conversations — when you can't think of a word, write it down. Look it up after.
  2. Your reading — articles, blog posts, documentation in English.
  3. Podcasts in your industry — vocab + pronunciation simultaneously.
  4. Native colleagues' messages — words and phrases they use that you don't.

Don't memorize random GRE word lists. They're useless for daily communication.

The acquisition method

  1. Encounter a new word in context.
  2. Look up the meaning AND example sentences.
  3. Note the part of speech (verb / noun / adjective / adverb).
  4. Note the pronunciation.
  5. Make it yours: use it in 3 sentences of your own.
  6. Reuse it within 24 hours, ideally out loud.
  7. Reuse it within a week.

Without active reuse, words don't stick. You need 7+ exposures + uses for a word to become active.

Spaced repetition

Apps like Anki / Quizlet show you words at increasing intervals. Effective for memorization. The trick: make cards with FULL CONTEXT, not just word → translation.

Card front:
"The CEO ___ that we need to refocus." (announce / declare / mention)

Card back:
"The CEO announced that we need to refocus."
- to announce = to make known publicly (formal)
- "She announced her resignation."

"Said" gets boring. Switch among:

Module 21
Active Listening

The myth: listening is passive

It's not. Active listening means processing what's said, signaling that you're processing, asking clarifying questions, and confirming understanding. It's exhausting and effective.

The 5 levels of listening

LevelWhat it looks like
1. IgnoringDistracted by phone / other thoughts
2. Pretending"Mhm," "yes," but not really hearing
3. SelectiveHearing only the parts you want
4. AttentiveHearing the words and intent
5. Empathic / ActiveHearing words, intent, emotion, AND showing it

The active listening behaviors

1. Eye contact (or camera contact on calls)

Without it, you appear distracted. Look AT the person speaking. On video calls: look at the camera, not your own image.

2. Paraphrasing

Repeat back what you heard, in your own words. Catches misunderstanding before it grows.

Useful phrases:
  • "So what you're saying is..."
  • "Let me make sure I got this right..."
  • "If I understand correctly, you mean..."
  • "In other words, ..."
  • "So basically..."

3. Asking clarifying questions

  • "What do you mean by X?"
  • "Can you give an example?"
  • "How does that work in practice?"
  • "What happens if Y?"

4. Acknowledgment sounds

"Mm-hm," "right," "I see," "okay," brief nods. Tell the speaker you're with them.

5. Pausing before responding

Don't jump in. A 1–2 second pause shows you considered what they said. Many non-natives rush to respond out of nervousness.

6. Summarizing at the end

"So to recap — you want X by Friday, and we'll handle Y next week. Did I get that right?" Confirms shared understanding before parting.

Common listening mistakes

  • Planning your reply while they're still speaking — you stop hearing.
  • Interrupting — even helpful corrections derail.
  • Topic-jumping — answering with your own related story instead of staying with theirs.
  • Filling silences — sometimes they need to think.
  • Premature advice — they often want to be heard, not fixed.

Listening for what's NOT said

Tone, hesitation, what they avoid — meaningful signals. "We'll see" usually means "I doubt it." Long pauses before "yes" suggest uncertainty. A senior colleague saying "interesting" can mean "I disagree."

Listening for the implicit question

People rarely state the question directly. "I've been trying to deploy for an hour" probably means "I need help" or "Can you check this?" Practice hearing the request inside the statement.

Module 22
Understanding Accents

The reality of international tech

You'll work with Americans, British, Australians, French, Germans, Brazilians, Chinese, Japanese, fellow Indians from different regions. Each has accent patterns. The skill isn't understanding ONE accent — it's training your ear to handle variety.

Strategy 1: exposure

The more accents you hear, the easier they become. Listen to:

  • American — most podcasts, Hollywood, US tech videos.
  • British — BBC, Doctor Who, Sherlock.
  • Australian — AFL podcasts, Australian YouTubers.
  • Indian (regional varieties) — Tamil, Bengali, Punjabi-influenced English.
  • Continental European — French, German, Spanish accents speaking English.
  • Asian — Chinese, Japanese, Korean speakers.

30 minutes daily over months. Your ear will adjust automatically.

Strategy 2: subtitles

Watch English content with English subtitles. Trains your ear to map sound to known words. Once comfortable, turn subtitles off.

Strategy 3: ask people to slow down

Politely. Phrases:

  • "Sorry, could you repeat that please?"
  • "Could you say that a bit slower?"
  • "I didn't quite catch the last part."
  • "Bear with me, I want to make sure I understood."

Asking for clarification is professional, not weak. Pretending to understand creates bigger problems.

Strategy 4: ask for written confirmation

After verbal discussions, "Could you send me a quick summary on Slack?" gives you the words to reread without the accent overlay.

Specific accents — the key features

American (General American)

  • "r" sound is strong (rhotic) — "car" has a clear r.
  • "t" between vowels often becomes a soft "d" — "water" sounds like "wadder."
  • Faster pace than British; heavier reductions.
  • "can" and "can't" sound similar; listen for context.

British (Received Pronunciation)

  • "r" not pronounced after vowels — "car" is "cah."
  • Crisper consonants.
  • "a" in "bath, can't, dance" is /ɑː/ (long ah).
  • More tonal variation.

Australian

  • Vowels shifted — "day" sounds like "die," "no" like "noy."
  • Sentence often ends with rising intonation even for statements (uptalk).
  • "G'day" instead of "good day."

Continental European

  • Often clearer (slower, syllable-timed) — easier for non-natives.
  • French: silent "h," "th" → "z."
  • German: "v" pronounced as "f," "w" as "v."

East Asian

  • "r" and "l" sometimes swapped (Japanese).
  • Chinese: tonal patterns may transfer; "th" becomes "s."
  • Korean: similar issues with initial consonant clusters.

Don't be embarrassed to ask

Even native speakers ask each other to repeat — different regional accents within English are tough. "Could you repeat that?" is a normal, professional question, not a sign of weakness.

Module 24
Overcoming Fear of Speaking

Where the fear comes from

  • Fear of judgment — they'll think I'm stupid because of my English.
  • Past failure — someone laughed at me / corrected me publicly.
  • Self-comparison — they sound so fluent; I never will.
  • Perfectionism — if I can't say it perfectly, I won't say it at all.
  • Context — Indian education often punishes mistakes; cultural deference avoids spotlight.

The truths that help

1. Listeners don't notice your grammar

They're busy understanding your point. Native speakers make grammar mistakes constantly. You're not being graded.

2. Imperfect speech is faster than perfect silence

If you don't speak, you don't improve. The path to fluency is through hundreds of imperfect conversations, not zero perfect ones.

3. Most senior people had your fear

Sundar Pichai, Satya Nadella, Indra Nooyi — all started with Indian-accented English they were sometimes embarrassed by. It didn't stop them.

4. Confidence is a learnable skill

It comes from EXPOSURE, not from feeling ready. You'll never feel ready. Speak anyway.

The practice ladder — start small

  1. Talk to yourself — narrate your day in English aloud. No pressure, no audience.
  2. Talk to AI — ChatGPT voice mode, language exchange apps. Patient, never judges.
  3. Talk to one supportive person — friend, partner, online tutor.
  4. Talk in small groups — 3–4 people you know.
  5. Speak in meetings — first ask questions, then share opinions, then propose ideas.
  6. Speak to clients / external stakeholders.
  7. Present — to small audiences first, then larger.
  8. Public speaking — the top of the ladder.

Stay one step above your comfort zone. Push, then stabilize, then push again.

Concrete techniques

The 5-second rule

If you have a thought to share in a meeting, count to 5 silently. If still relevant, say it. The pause feels long but is barely noticeable to others. Many people stay silent because they overthink past the moment.

Pre-script the first sentence

Hardest is starting. Have a few openers ready:

  • "I'd like to add one thing..."
  • "Quick question — ..."
  • "Building on what Alice said..."
  • "From my side, ..."
  • "To play devil's advocate, ..."

The recovery line

When you stumble, you don't have to pretend it didn't happen. Practiced lines for recovery:

  • "Let me rephrase that..."
  • "What I mean is..."
  • "Sorry, my point is..."
  • "Actually, scratch that — what I want to say is..."

These signal "I'm correcting myself" — totally normal, native speakers do it constantly.

Recover the word you forgot

  • "What's the word... when you ___" (describe it)
  • "It's like a... [paraphrase]"
  • "It begins with /th/..." (phonetic clue)
  • "Anyway, you know what I mean — moving on."

Listeners will fill in the word for you. That's the most natural part of conversation.

The growth curve

Confidence grows non-linearly. You'll have months of feeling stuck, then suddenly notice you spoke for 10 minutes without thinking about your English. The plateau is part of the process. Don't quit during it.

The fluency exercises that work

1. The 4-3-2 technique

Pick a topic (your job, your weekend, an opinion). Speak for 4 minutes. Same topic, 3 minutes. Same topic, 2 minutes. Each iteration forces you to be more efficient. Increases speed and reduces hesitation.

2. Shadowing

Already covered (Module 8). Listen + repeat simultaneously. Most powerful single exercise for fluency + pronunciation.

3. Output journaling — speak out loud

Talk to yourself for 5–10 minutes about anything. Your day, an idea, code you're writing. Don't worry about errors. The goal is moving from thought → speech without translation.

4. Watch and re-tell

Watch a 5-minute clip. Pause. Tell yourself what happened, in English, in your own words. Builds the storytelling muscle.

5. Diary in spoken English

End-of-day audio diary, 2 minutes, no editing. After a month, listen to your old entries. Hear the progress. Spot recurring errors to work on.

The "thinking in English" myth

You don't need to "stop thinking in your language." Most fluent multilinguals translate quickly. The trick is making translation faster — through repetition, not magic. Eventually, common phrases come out without conscious translation. That's fluency.

Reduce dependency on the perfect word

Fluency suffers when you stop to find the precise word. Train substitution:

  • If you don't know "scrutinize," say "look at carefully."
  • If you don't know "discrepancy," say "difference that doesn't make sense."
  • If you don't know "implementation," say "how we built it."

The simpler word never wrong; the precise word, sometimes inaccurate.

  • ChatGPT voice mode — patient AI conversation partner; great for everyday topics.
  • Toastmasters — local clubs; structured speaking practice.
  • Discord communities / Twitter Spaces in your interest area.

Module 26
Pausing & Pacing

Why pacing matters more than vocabulary

A speaker with average vocabulary and excellent pacing is easier to follow than a speaker with great vocabulary speaking too fast.

The international speaking pace

  • Average native English: ~150 words/minute.
  • Comfortable for non-native listeners: ~120–130 words/minute.
  • For presentations / important content: ~110 words/minute.

Most non-native speakers speak too fast when nervous. Slow down deliberately for clarity.

The power of the pause

Pauses do work that words can't:

  • Mark the end of a thought.
  • Let listeners process.
  • Add emphasis to what came before or comes after.
  • Show confidence — only secure speakers pause.
  • Replace fillers ("um," "like," "you know").

Where to pause

SituationPause length
Within a sentence (after key word)0.5 sec
Between sentences1 sec
Between paragraphs / topics2 sec
Before an important point ("...And here's the thing — [pause] — we don't have time")1–2 sec
After a question to a group3–5 sec (feels long, isn't)
When asked a question, before answering1 sec — shows thought

The "comma rule"

When speaking, treat written commas as pause cues. "Yesterday[,] after the deploy[,] we noticed[,] that p99 latency spiked[.]" Even tiny pauses make this much clearer than blurted-out "Yesterdayafterthedeploywenoticedthatp99latencyspiked."

Volume modulation

Don't speak at the same volume the whole time. Important points: slightly louder. Asides: slightly quieter. The variation keeps listeners engaged.

Specific pacing for different contexts

Technical explanations

Slower. Add pauses after introducing new terms. Listeners need processing time for technical content even in their native language.

Storytelling

Variable pace. Build slowly to the point; speed up at the action; pause at the climax.

Stand-ups

Brisker, but still with pauses between yesterday/today/blockers. One run-on sentence loses everyone.

Presentations

Slower than conversation. Pause after each slide title. Pause for emphasis on key conclusions.

The exercise to internalize this

Pick a 1-minute speech you've recorded. Re-record it 30% slower. Compare. Most people are shocked by how much clearer the slow version is. Then aim for that pace in real conversations.

Reducing Fillers

Why fillers hurt

  • Distract from your point.
  • Signal nervousness.
  • Make you sound less authoritative.
  • Add length without value.

Why we use them

Fillers buy time when our brain is searching for the next word. We're afraid silence sounds like we're stuck. Actually, silence sounds confident. Fillers sound stuck.

The replacement: silent pause

When tempted to say "um," close your mouth instead. The 1-second silence feels eternal to you, but listeners barely notice it. Try this: record yourself saying "Yesterday um I was uh working on the bug." Now record "Yesterday [pause] I was [pause] working on the bug." The second sounds vastly more confident.

Step-by-step reduction plan

  1. Awareness — record yourself in a real conversation or meeting. Count fillers. Most people are shocked.
  2. Identify your top 1–2 — usually one or two dominate.
  3. Substitute with silence — practice replacing them deliberately.
  4. Tap a pen — old speech-coach trick. Each filler, tap. Builds awareness fast.
  5. Re-record weekly — measure your progress.

Useful "intentional" pauses to replace fillers

Instead of "um/uh," use:

  • (silent pause)
  • "Let me think for a second."
  • "That's a good question."
  • "To be honest..."
  • "Hmm."

Even a "hmm" sounds more thoughtful than "um."

The "so" addiction

Many engineers start every sentence with "So." Once or twice fine. Every sentence: distracting. Replace with:

  • Direct start: "I deployed the fix."
  • "Right." or "OK." or "Now,"
  • Just nothing. Start the actual sentence.

Realistic expectations

You won't eliminate fillers. Native speakers use them too. The goal is reduction — from a "um" every 5 seconds to one every 30 seconds. That's the difference between distracted listening and engaged listening.

Module 28
Speaking Off the Cuff

What "off the cuff" means

Speaking spontaneously without preparation. The hardest scenario for non-native speakers. Sudden questions, "what do you think?" moments, calls without warning, networking events.

The fear of these moments

The brain freezes. You scramble for the right word in the right grammar in the right tone. Result: a stumbling answer or silence. The trick is to have frameworks ready that buy you time and give you structure.

Framework 1: PREP

For sharing an opinion or making a point:

  • Point — your conclusion in one sentence.
  • Reason — why you think so.
  • Example — a concrete instance.
  • Point — restate your conclusion.

Example: "I think we should postpone the migration. [P] We're stretched thin this sprint. [R] Last week we had three production incidents already. [E] So I'd push it to next sprint when we have more capacity. [P]"

Framework 2: STAR

For describing experience or telling stories (great for interviews):

  • Situation — context.
  • Task — what needed to be done.
  • Action — what you did.
  • Result — what happened.

Framework 3: Past — Present — Future

For status updates or reflections:

  • Past: what happened recently.
  • Present: where things stand now.
  • Future: what's next.

Framework 4: Pros and cons

For decisions:

  • "On one hand... on the other hand..."
  • "The benefit would be... the risk is..."
  • "Going forward, we have a few options: A, B, C..."

Buying time phrases — when caught off guard

  • "That's a great question. Let me think for a moment."
  • "Off the top of my head, I'd say..."
  • "Let me approach this from a different angle..."
  • "I'd want to dig into the details before committing, but my initial thought is..."
  • "I haven't fully thought through this, but here's my first reaction..."
  • "Could you give me a second to gather my thoughts?"
  • "Let me come back to you on this — give me an hour to check, and I'll Slack you."

The "I don't know" toolkit

Saying "I don't know" professionally:

  • "I'm not sure — let me find out and get back to you."
  • "I don't have that information offhand. Can I confirm and follow up?"
  • "That's outside my expertise — Bob would know."
  • "My honest answer is: I'm not sure. Let me look into it."

Pretending to know is far worse than admitting you don't. Confidence is fine without expertise; faking expertise destroys credibility.

Practice: 1-minute impromptu

Daily exercise:

  1. Pick a random topic — "remote work," "the best programming language," "your hometown."
  2. Set a 1-minute timer.
  3. Speak continuously, no preparation.
  4. Use one of the frameworks (PREP, STAR, Past/Present/Future).

30 days of this transforms how you handle surprise questions.

Why storytelling matters at work

Engineers who tell good stories get promoted faster. Why? Because:

  • Selling ideas requires a narrative arc, not just data.
  • Networking depends on memorable stories about who you are.
  • Interviews are 50% storytelling.
  • Status updates are micro-stories.
  • Conflict resolution requires recasting events as story.

The basic story arc

              ▲
   Tension    │
   builds   ╱
          ╱
        ╱
      ╱ Set-up
    ╱

Every good story has: setup → tension → resolution.

The STAR pattern (work-friendly)

  • Situation: where, when, who.
  • Task: what needed to be done.
  • Action: what you/team did.
  • Result: outcome + lessons.

Used heavily in interviews. Also great for retrospectives and lessons-learned shares.

Example STAR story

"[S] Last quarter, our login latency was creeping up — p99 went from 200 ms to nearly a second. [T] We needed to figure out the cause and fix it before it affected customers. [A] I led an investigation, traced it to a slow query on the user-rights table that was missing an index. We added a covering index in a maintenance window and rolled it out. [R] Latency dropped back below 200 ms, and we caught the issue before any customer noticed. The retro recommended adding query-perf monitoring earlier in the chain — we did that next sprint."

Note: 90 seconds, vivid, complete arc, lesson at the end.

What makes work stories good

  • Specific — "p99 went from 200 ms to nearly a second" beats "it was slow."
  • Personal — what YOU did and learned.
  • Honest — include what went wrong.
  • Lesson — explicit takeaway.

Storytelling for "tell me about yourself"

The most-asked interview question. Don't list your resume. Tell a 2-minute story:

  1. Where you're coming from now.
  2. The path that got you there (1–2 key moments).
  3. Where you want to go next.

Connect it to why you're talking to this person/company.

Even a stand-up update can use mini-narrative:

Boring list

Watch for cultural style differences

  • Indian style — often starts with context, builds slowly. Audience may lose patience.
  • American style — leads with the punchline, then explains. "We hit a snag yesterday — let me tell you what happened..."

For international audiences: start with the conclusion, then the story. Listeners decide whether to follow you based on the first 5 seconds.

Practice exercise

  1. Pick a recent work event (bug, deploy, project win/loss).
  2. Time yourself telling it in 60 seconds.
  3. Use STAR.
  4. Re-tell it in 30 seconds.
  5. Re-tell it in 2 minutes with more detail.

Same story, three lengths. Master this and you can adapt to any situation.

Module 33
Disagreeing Politely

Why this is high-stakes

Wrong: agreeing when you disagree → bad decisions get made.

Wrong: disagreeing aggressively → reputation suffers; trust erodes.

Right: disagreeing while preserving the relationship → better decisions, stronger trust.

The tone formula

Diplomatic disagreement = acknowledge their point + add a hedge + offer your view + invite discussion.

Phrases for polite disagreement

Soft disagreement:

  • "I see your point, but I think..."
  • "That's a fair view. I'm leaning a bit differently though..."
  • "I might be missing something, but..."
  • "I'm not sure I agree completely. Could we look at...?"
  • "You make a good point about X. I do have some concerns about Y..."
  • "That's interesting. Have we considered...?"

Stronger disagreement (when needed):

  • "I have to push back on that. Here's why..."
  • "I respectfully disagree. My concern is..."
  • "I see this very differently — let me explain my thinking..."
  • "I have a different read. Mind if I share?"

Keep it about ideas, not people

Personal attack

"You're wrong."
"You don't understand the system."
"That's a stupid idea."
"Why would you even suggest that?"

Idea-focused

"That approach has some risks. Here's
what worries me..."

"I think the design might miss this
edge case — let me share."

"I'd push for a different approach
because of X."

The Indian-English challenge

Many Indian engineers were raised to defer to seniority. Disagreeing with a manager or senior in public can feel disrespectful. International tech culture (especially American/Dutch) expects disagreement at all levels. Silence is read as agreement; failure to push back is seen as weakness or disengagement.

Reframe: disagreeing professionally IS respect. You're treating the other person as capable of handling honest exchange.

The "yes, and" / "yes, but" trap

  • "Yes, but..." — sounds like dismissing their point. The "but" cancels what came before.
  • "Yes, and..." — builds on their point. Often works better.
  • "That's true, AND I'd add..." — preserves their idea while extending.

Disagreeing with a senior person

  • Privately first if it's a major call.
  • Frame as a question: "I want to understand — why X over Y?"
  • Provide concrete reasoning, not just feelings.
  • Acknowledge their authority: "You may have context I don't, but here's my concern."
  • Accept the final call gracefully if they overrule.

The "disagree and commit" principle

Amazon's leadership principle. Once a decision is made — even one you disagreed with — commit fully. Don't sabotage by half-supporting. State your view openly during the discussion; then row in the agreed direction afterward.

Saying you were wrong

Easy phrases (which sound senior, not weak):

  • "You're right, I missed that."
  • "Good point — I'll revise my view."
  • "That's a better way to think about it."
  • "I take that back."

Owning errors quickly builds more credibility than defending bad positions.

You don't have to justify why. "I have to head out" is enough. "I have a meeting" is also fine. You don't owe a calendar.

Closes the loop, deepens the connection, and reminds them of you.

Module 36
Diplomatic Language

What "diplomatic" means in corporate English

Same message, softer wrapping. Used to deliver hard truths without provoking defensive reactions. The skill that distinguishes senior from junior communicators.

The softening toolbox

1. Hedge words

Maybe, might, could, would, perhaps, possibly, somewhat, slightly, a bit, kind of.

  • "This is wrong." → "This might be a bit off."
  • "It will fail." → "It could run into issues."
  • "You're behind schedule." → "We seem to be a bit behind schedule."

2. Questions instead of statements

  • "You should test this." → "Have you had a chance to test this?"
  • "You missed a step." → "Did we cover step X?"
  • "This won't work." → "Have we considered what happens if Y?"

3. "I" instead of "you"

  • "You're confusing me." → "I'm having trouble following — could you clarify?"
  • "You forgot to..." → "I noticed X wasn't included — let me know."
  • "You're wrong." → "I'm seeing it differently — let me share my view."

4. Passive voice (for blame-neutral observations)

  • "You broke the build." → "The build seems to have broken."
  • "You missed the deadline." → "The deadline was missed."

Use sparingly — overuse makes you sound evasive. But for moments where the goal is the FACT not the BLAME, passive helps.

5. The "I'd love" / "I'd appreciate" frame

  • "I'd love to see..." (instead of "you should...")
  • "I'd appreciate it if..." (instead of "please do...")
  • "It would be helpful if..." (instead of "do this...")

6. Acknowledgment + concern

"I see why you'd think that, AND here's my concern..." Always validates first.

The diplomatic gradient — same message, increasing intensity

  1. "There's maybe a small issue here..."
  2. "I see one concern: ..."
  3. "I think we have a problem with X."
  4. "This is a problem we need to solve."
  5. "This is broken; we have to fix it before we ship."

Match intensity to severity AND to your relationship with the person.

Diplomatic feedback

Direct (American/Dutch can handle; many won't)

Cultural calibration

AudienceDiplomatic level

Default to medium-high diplomatic. You can always be more direct later if the relationship/culture invites it.

The trap: too much diplomacy

Over-softening hides the message. "There might possibly be perhaps a small concern with this approach if we look at it carefully" → nobody knows you actually disagree. Sometimes you need to be clear:

  • "I want to be direct: this won't meet the deadline."
  • "To be honest, I'm worried about X."
  • "I have to push back on this."

Diplomacy is the default; clarity overrides when stakes are high.

Module 39
Giving Feedback

The two failure modes

  • Too soft: vague, not actionable, no improvement happens.
  • Too harsh: damaging, defensive, no improvement happens.

Useful feedback is specific, behavioral, kind, and actionable.

The SBI model

LetterMeaningExample
SituationWhen/where it happened"In yesterday's design review..."
BehaviorWhat they did (observable)"...you interrupted Alice three times."
ImpactThe effect on you/team"It made the discussion feel one-sided, and I noticed Alice stopped contributing after that."

Avoids labeling the person ("you're rude"). Focuses on a specific event, the behavior, the impact.

Positive feedback also benefits from SBI

"Hey Bob, in yesterday's stand-up, when you flagged the SLA risk early, it gave the team time to mitigate before it escalated. That kind of proactive call-out is really valuable. Keep doing it."

Specific praise lands. Vague praise ("great job!") doesn't tell them what to repeat.

The feedback sandwich — proceed with caution

The traditional model: positive — negative — positive. Many people see through it and discount even the genuine positives. Better:

  • State the purpose: "I want to share some feedback to help with [goal]."
  • Be direct, kind, specific.
  • End with offer of help: "Let me know how I can support you on this."

Constructive criticism examples

Setup phrases:

  • "I have some feedback on the doc you shared — is now a good time?"
  • "Got a minute? I want to share an observation."
  • "I noticed something I wanted to flag — happy to talk through it."

Specific feedback:

  • "In the meeting, I felt like the technical depth got lost. A diagram up front might have helped — what do you think?"
  • "Your PR descriptions have been short lately — adding context about WHY would help reviewers."
  • "I noticed your last three deploys broke staging. Maybe we slow down the rollout cadence?"

Code review feedback

Code review is feedback all day. The patterns:

Bad styleBetter style
"This is wrong.""This seems off — could you double-check whether X happens when Y?"
"Refactor this.""This function's getting complex — would it make sense to split out the validation part?"
"You missed error handling.""What happens here if the DB times out? I think we'd hit an unhandled exception."
"Bad name.""What about renaming `data` to `userOrders` for clarity?"

The "nit" / "suggestion" / "blocking" hierarchy

Modern code review etiquette uses prefixes:

  • nit: minor stylistic preference; don't have to address.
  • suggestion: would be nice but not blocking.
  • question: trying to understand, not requiring change.
  • (unprefixed): needs to be addressed before merge.

Reduces friction; reviewers don't have to negotiate every comment's importance.

Cultural sensitivity in giving feedback

  • Praise publicly, criticize privately. Always.
  • For Indian / East Asian colleagues: especially important to preserve face. Heavier on private channels.
  • For American/Dutch/Israeli: more direct is welcomed; over-softening can come across as evasive.
  • Never give feedback when you're emotional. Wait an hour.

Module 40
Receiving Feedback

The natural reactions to avoid

  1. Defending — "But the reason I did that was..."
  2. Counter-attacking — "Well, you also..."
  3. Agreeing too quickly — "You're right, I'm terrible." (deflects but doesn't process)
  4. Discounting — "They don't really get it."
  5. Fixating on tone — "Why did they say it that way?"

The good response

  1. Listen fully without interrupting.
  2. Ask clarifying questions if needed: "Could you give me an example?"
  3. Thank them — "Thanks for telling me. I appreciate the feedback."
  4. Take a beat — don't promise immediate change in the heat of the moment.
  5. Reflect later — even uncomfortable feedback usually has 30%+ truth.
  6. Follow up — "I've been thinking about what you said. Here's what I plan to change."

Useful phrases when receiving feedback

When you need to understand:

  • "Could you give me a specific example?"
  • "What would have looked different?"
  • "Help me understand the impact."

When you receive it:

  • "Thanks for telling me — I'll think on this."
  • "I appreciate you sharing that."
  • "That's hard to hear, but useful. Thank you."
  • "I want to take a day to sit with it before responding."

When you've had time to process:

  • "You were right. Here's what I'm changing..."
  • "I've thought about it — I see the part about X. I'm less sure about Y. Could we talk?"
  • "I disagree with part of it, but I see the concern. Here's how I'm thinking about it now..."

Asking for feedback

Most people give better feedback when asked. Don't wait for performance review.

  • "What's one thing I could improve?"
  • "What would make me more effective in this role?"
  • "What's something you wish I did differently?"
  • "How could I have handled that meeting better?"

Ask peers, manager, direct reports (if you have them). The 360 view tells you more than any one perspective.

The "stop / start / continue" framework

Useful when explicitly asking for feedback:

  1. What should I stop doing?
  2. What should I start doing?
  3. What should I continue doing?

The "continue" question matters — without it, people only think of negatives. Hearing what you should keep doing is useful too.

Handling unfair feedback

Sometimes feedback is wrong. Reactions:

  • Don't dismiss in the moment. Listen, thank.
  • Reflect later. Is there 10% truth even if 90% is off?
  • If still convinced it's wrong: have a follow-up conversation. "I've been thinking about your feedback. I see your perspective on X, but I disagree on Y, and here's why..."
  • If patterns emerge from multiple sources: trust the data, even if individual instances feel unfair.

The growth mindset framing

Carol Dweck's research: people with a "growth mindset" treat feedback as data for improvement, not as judgment of worth. Feedback isn't about who you ARE; it's about specific behaviors that can change. Internalize this and feedback becomes useful, not threatening.

Module 41
Saying No

Why saying no is hard

  • You don't want to seem unhelpful.
  • You worry about consequences (reputation, career, relationship).
  • Cultures (Indian, Japanese) may not have direct "no" phrasing.
  • You hope something will work out.

But: saying yes to everything → over-commitment → missed deadlines → real damage to reputation. Saying no clearly preserves quality and trust.

The vague no — Indian-English habit

PhraseWhat it means in Indian EnglishWhat international hears
"I'll try.""I probably can't but I don't want to say no.""I'll do it."
"Let's see.""Probably no.""Maybe yes."
"Maybe.""Probably no.""50/50."
"I'm a bit busy.""No.""Slightly inconvenient but possible."
(silence / nodding)"I don't agree but I'm not refusing.""Yes, agreed."

International colleagues often miss your "no" because you're too polite. Then they're surprised when you don't deliver. Result: you LOOK unreliable when you were just trying to be polite.

Direct "no" phrases for international audiences

Soft but clear no:

  • "I can't take that on right now — my plate's full."
  • "I won't be able to do that this week. Can it wait?"
  • "That's outside what I can commit to."
  • "I have to say no to this one."
  • "I don't think I can do that and meet my other deadlines."

No with alternative:

  • "I can't do it this week, but I could pick it up next week."
  • "I can't take the whole thing, but I can help with [smaller piece]."
  • "I'm not the right person for this — Bob has the context."
  • "I can't make that meeting; could we do async via Slack?"

Pushing back on scope:

  • "To do X by Friday, we'd need to drop Y. Which is more important?"
  • "That's a big scope addition — let's discuss the trade-off."
  • "I can do this, but it'll push Z back by a week."

The trade-off response — your most powerful tool

Instead of "no," frame it as a trade-off. The other person decides:

"Yes, I can take this on. To do it by Friday, I'd have to deprioritize the migration. Want me to do that?"

Forces the requester to decide which is actually more important. Often they realize their request wasn't urgent, or they reset their own expectations.

Saying no to your manager

Hardest case. Cultural deference makes it harder. But:

  • Managers need honest pushback to make good decisions.
  • Saying yes and missing is worse than saying no.
  • Frame as concern + alternative: "I'm worried we can't deliver X without compromising Y. Can we discuss?"

Specific phrases:

  • "I want to be honest — I don't think I can deliver this and the other thing on time."
  • "I'm at capacity. Can we look at what to deprioritize?"
  • "I'm not sure I'm the right person for this — let's talk about who is."

Saying no to a request you don't want to do at all

  • "I'm going to pass on this one."
  • "That's not something I'm interested in taking on."
  • "This isn't a fit for me right now."

You don't always need a reason. "I can't" is a complete sentence.

Avoid over-explaining

The instinct is to defend your no with reasons. Sometimes that opens negotiation. "I can't because I have ABC" → other person tries to remove ABC. Cleaner: state the no, briefly explain context, end.

The follow-up

If you say yes to a small piece while saying no to the rest, follow up to confirm:

"To recap — I'll do X this week. The Y piece will need someone else or wait until next sprint. Let me know if that works."

Email Structure

Email is the most measurable thing you write at work. Unlike spoken words, emails get forwarded, archived, screenshotted, and quoted years later. A clear email saves three back-and-forths. A sloppy email costs your reputation.

The five-part structure

  1. Subject line — what this email is about, in 8 words or fewer
  2. Greeting — Hi [Name], / Hello team, / Dear [Name],
  3. Purpose (first sentence) — why you're writing
  4. Body — context, details, ask
  5. Close — clear next step, sign-off, name

Lead with the ask

The single biggest improvement to your emails: put the ask in the first sentence. Background goes after.

Buried ask

"Hi John, As you may recall, last quarter we discussed the migration project and the various stakeholders involved. The team has been working hard on this and we have made significant progress over the past several weeks. Considering all of this, I wanted to ask if you could approve the budget extension."

Front-loaded ask

"Hi John, Could you approve a $5K budget extension for the migration project? Background: we hit two unexpected vendor costs in week 6 — details below."

Tone calibration

Match the recipient and the channel. A note to your manager is not a court filing.

  • Casual (peer, internal): "Hey Mike, can you take a look at the PR when you get a sec? No rush."
  • Standard (cross-team): "Hi Priya, could you review the API spec by Wednesday? Happy to walk through it on a call if easier."
  • Formal (external client, executives): "Hello Mr. Chen, Following up on our Tuesday discussion about the integration timeline. We've completed the scoping and are ready to share findings."
Length test: If your email is over 200 words, it should probably be a doc + a short email saying "see doc." Long emails don't get read; they get filed.

Module 43
Subject Lines

The subject line is the most-read part of your email. People decide in two seconds whether to open it now, later, or never. A good subject line is a tiny abstract of the email.

Three rules for subject lines

  1. Specific over general: "Update" → "Sprint 23 status: 4 of 5 stories done"
  2. Action over topic: "Auth feature" → "Approval needed: Auth deploy Friday"
  3. Front-load the most important word: people scan the first 3-4 words

  • "Hi"
  • "Quick question"
  • "Update"
  • "Important"
  • "Meeting"
  • "Please read"
  • "FYI"
  • "(no subject)"

Specific

  • "Pricing question on Enterprise plan"
  • "Question: Can I get prod DB read access?"
  • "Update: Sprint 23 closed, 18 of 20 pts shipped"
  • "Important: SSL cert expires Sun, action needed"
  • "Meeting request: 30 min API review Tue"
  • "Please review: PR #482 (auth refactor)"
  • "FYI: Postgres upgrade to 15 next Friday"

  • [ACTION] — recipient must do something
  • [FYI] — no reply expected
  • [DECISION] — needs a yes/no/choice
  • [BLOCKED] — blocking your work
  • [EXTERNAL] — for security; some companies auto-tag
The reply-to-self test: would you open this email at 4:55pm on a Friday with 47 other unread emails? If not, fix the subject.

Module 45
Mistakes to Avoid

The patterns below are how good emails go bad. Most are fixable in seconds once you spot them.

1. Reply-all when you mean reply

Easy mistake, hard to undo. Before sending: glance at the recipient list. Does this really need to go to all 30 people? If it's a side comment to one person, hit reply, not reply-all.

2. The wall of text

Long emails don't get read carefully — they get scanned. If you have more than 3 paragraphs, use:

  • Headers for each section
  • Bullets for lists
  • Bold for the action items
  • A TL;DR at the top if longer than 200 words

3. Burying the ask

If your email has a request, the request goes in the subject line and the first sentence. Background after.

4. Multiple asks in one email

One email = one ask. If you're asking for budget approval AND a meeting AND a doc review, that's three emails. Bundling them means at least one gets forgotten.

5. Vague deadlines

Vague

  • "At the earliest"
  • "As soon as possible"
  • "When you get a chance"
  • "By EOD" (whose timezone?)

Specific

  • "By Friday Nov 14"
  • "By 5pm IST Wednesday"
  • "By end of next week is fine"
  • "Today if possible, tomorrow at the latest"

6. Ambiguous "you"

If you address an email to 5 people and write "could you review by Friday?" — who does that mean? Everyone assumes someone else. Name names: "Priya, could you review by Friday?"

7. CC abuse

  • To: people who need to do something or reply
  • CC: people who need to know but don't need to act
  • BCC: mass announcements where you're protecting addresses, or "loop you out of further replies"

CC'ing your boss to pressure someone is transparent and damages trust. Don't.

8. Sending when emotional

If you're angry or frustrated, write the email — then save it as a draft and revisit in 4 hours. Almost every "send when angry" email becomes a regret.

9. The "just" trap

"Just checking in", "just wanted to ask", "just following up" — softens you out of authority. Once or twice is fine; in every sentence it makes you sound apologetic.

  • "Just wondering if you had any thoughts on the proposal" → meek
  • "Wanted to get your thoughts on the proposal" → confident, still polite

10. Forgetting the attachment

If your email mentions an attachment, attach it before writing the body. Or use a Gmail/Outlook plugin that warns when "attached" appears with no attachment.

11. Not proofreading

Every email gets one read-through before sending. Catch:

  • Wrong recipient name
  • "Mr/Ms" placeholders never filled in
  • Old subject from a forwarded chain
  • Numbers/dates that should be checked
  • Tone that reads sharper than you intended

12. Auto-translated phrases

Phrases that translate literally from Hindi/regional language to English often confuse international readers:

TranslatedMeans in international EnglishUse instead

"Prepone"(not a word)"Move earlier"
"Out of station"(unclear — out of train station?)"Out of office"
"Cousin brother"(your cousin, not your brother)"Cousin"
"Same to same"(unclear)"Identical / the same"
"Pass out (from college)"(faint)"Graduate"
"Updation"(not a word)"Update" (as a noun too)

Module 47
Meeting Etiquette

Meetings are the most expensive thing your company does. Six people in a one-hour meeting is six hours of paid attention. Treat that time with respect — both yours and theirs.

Before the meeting

  • Read the agenda. If there's no agenda, ask "what are we trying to decide?" before accepting.
  • Read the pre-read. If a doc was shared, read it. Showing up unprepared wastes everyone else's time.
  • Be on time. "On time" means joined and ready 30 seconds before start, not arriving as the host says hello.
  • Test your audio/video for any new tool. Don't be the person who spends 5 minutes saying "can you hear me?"

During the meeting

  • Camera on (default) — unless the team norm is camera-off. Especially for small meetings <6 people.
  • Mute when not speaking. Background noise is everyone's problem.
  • Don't multitask visibly. Looking down at another screen reads as "you don't matter."
  • Don't eat on camera. Coffee is fine; lunch is not.
  • Don't dominate. If you've spoken three times in a row, pause and ask "what does the rest of the team think?"
  • Don't disappear either. If you've said nothing in 30 minutes, jump in — even just to acknowledge a point.

The Indian-English etiquette gap

Indian workplace culture often emphasizes deference to seniority. International tech meetings are flatter — expectations are different:

  • You don't have to say "Sir" or "Madam" — first names are normal even with executives
  • You don't have to wait until called on — interrupting politely is normal
  • You're expected to disagree with seniors when you have data — silence reads as "no opinion"
  • You're expected to push back on bad ideas, even from your manager — gently, but clearly

This isn't about copying foreign culture; it's about knowing what's expected when you join a meeting where defaults differ from what you grew up with.

The hand-raise

Most video tools have a "raise hand" button. Use it instead of jumping in if a meeting is large. For 4-6 person meetings, just speak up at a natural pause.

Side-channel chat

Most meetings have a chat sidebar. Use it for:

  • Sharing links the speaker references
  • Asking quick questions without interrupting
  • Clarifying spelling of names
  • "+1" reactions to support a point without speaking

Don't use it for: side jokes, off-topic chatter, or anything you wouldn't say to the whole room.

End-of-meeting recap

Before the meeting ends, the host (or you, if no one else does) should ask:

  • "What did we decide?"
  • "Who owns each action item?"
  • "By when?"

If a meeting ends without these three questions answered, it was a chat — not a meeting.

Module 48
Speaking Up in Meetings

Many Indian engineers stay quiet in meetings — not because they have nothing to say, but because they're calibrating: "is this the right moment? Is my point smart enough? Will I sound rude?" By the time you decide, the topic has moved on.

Speaking up is a skill you build. It's not about being the loudest; it's about contributing the perspective only you can give.

The 3-second rule

When the host opens the floor or there's a natural pause, count "one, two, three". If no one else speaks by three, jump in. The pause is your window.

Phrases to enter the conversation

  • "Can I add to that?"
  • "One thing to flag —"
  • "I had a thought on this —"
  • "Just to build on what [Name] said —"
  • "From the engineering side, one consideration is —"
  • "Quick observation —"
  • "I want to push back on that gently —"
  • "Sorry to interrupt — could I ask a clarifying question?"

What to actually say

Most useful contributions in meetings fall into a few buckets:

  1. Asking the dumb question — "Sorry, what does [acronym] mean here?" Half the room is grateful.
  2. Surfacing a risk — "One concern: this assumes the API is up. What's our fallback if it's down?"
  3. Connecting to data — "Worth noting that last quarter we tried this and saw [result]."
  4. Translating — "So if I'm hearing this right, the proposal is [X]?" — useful for surfacing misunderstandings.
  5. Calling for decision — "We've been on this for 20 minutes. What's our call?"
  6. Naming the elephant — "Are we all comfortable with the timeline? It feels tight."

The clarifying question

If you're nervous about contributing, start with clarifying questions. They're zero-risk — you're not putting forward an opinion, just making sure you understand. After 2-3, you'll find your voice for actual contributions.

Disagreeing in front of seniors

Indian culture often discourages disagreeing with managers in public. International tech culture often expects it (within limits). The compromise: disagree with the idea, not the person.

  • "I see it slightly differently — [your view]"
  • "Could I push back on that?"
  • "I want to make sure we've considered the alternative —"
  • "Playing devil's advocate for a moment —"
  • "I'm not sure I agree, here's why —"

If you got cut off

If someone interrupted you mid-sentence:

  • "Sorry, I wasn't quite finished — let me complete the thought."
  • "Hold on — let me finish my point and then I'd love to hear yours."
  • (Wait for the next pause) "Coming back to what I was saying earlier —"

Reclaiming the floor is normal and not rude.

If you don't understand what was said

Don't pretend. Pretending leads to delivering the wrong thing two weeks later. Phrases:

  • "Could you repeat that? I missed the last part."
  • "Sorry — can you give me an example of what you mean?"
  • "Just so I'm clear: are you saying [your interpretation]?"
  • "Could you spell out [acronym]? I'm not familiar with it."

Module 49
Disagreeing in Meetings

Disagreement done well moves the meeting forward. Disagreement done poorly creates politics. The difference is mostly tone, structure, and timing.

The disagreement framework

  1. Validate — show you heard them ("That's a fair point...")
  2. Pivot — signal a different view ("...however, I see it slightly differently.")
  3. Position — state your view with reasoning
  4. Open — leave space for response ("What do you think?")

Soft disagreement (most situations)

  • "That's interesting — I see it a bit differently. From the engineering side, [reason]."
  • "I hear what you're saying. My concern is [specific concern]."
  • "I want to push back on that. The data we saw last quarter showed [counter-evidence]."
  • "I'd argue the opposite — [your position] — because [reason]."
  • "Could I offer a different perspective?"

Strong disagreement (when it matters)

Sometimes you need to disagree firmly — for example, when production safety, security, or honesty is at stake.

  • "I have to disagree with this. [Reason]. I can't sign off on it as is."
  • "I want to be direct: this isn't a good idea, and here's why — [reason]."
  • "I think we'd be making a mistake to do this. The risk is [specific risk]."

Strong disagreement is a tool. Use it rarely. If you disagree firmly with everything, no one will listen when it actually matters.

Disagreeing with your manager

Managers in international tech culture generally want to be disagreed with — quietly, when needed. The standard rule: disagree privately, commit publicly.

  • If you can pull your manager aside (DM, 1:1, hallway), raise concerns there first.
  • If the topic is a public meeting decision, raise it once politely. If they overrule, accept it.
  • Once a decision is made, support it externally — even if you disagreed.

Disagree-and-commit

Common in tech culture. The phrase:

"I disagree with this approach, and I've shared why. But I understand the decision has been made — I'll commit to making it work."

This is honest, professional, and doesn't make you look stubborn.

What NOT to do

  • Don't agree in the meeting and complain after. If you have concerns, raise them in the meeting.
  • Don't stay silent when you have data. Silence reads as agreement.
  • Don't make it personal. "That's a stupid idea" → "I see issues with that approach."
  • Don't sigh, eye-roll, or smirk. Body language disagreement is the most damaging — it shows everyone you disagree but won't say so.
  • Don't pile on. If 3 people have already disagreed, you don't need to. Add new info, not new emphasis.

When disagreement feels personal

If someone disagrees with your idea sharply, it's not a personal attack — it's a sign they take the idea seriously enough to push on it. The worst feedback is silence; disagreement is engagement. Receive it, ask questions, don't get defensive.

  • "Help me understand your concern."
  • "What would change your mind?"
  • "What am I missing?"

Module 50
Running a Meeting

Running a meeting is a different skill from attending one. Most meetings are run badly. If you can run a good 30-minute meeting, your career accelerates — managers notice quickly.

The meeting checklist

0:00Start within 30 seconds of scheduled time

The opening

Bad opening: "OK so um... let's get started I guess... uh... so today we're going to talk about... a few things..."

Good opening: "Hi everyone, thanks for joining. We have 30 minutes. The goal today is to decide whether we're going with REST or GraphQL for the v2 API. By the end I want a decision and an owner. Let's start with [Name] walking through the proposal."

Three things in one sentence: time, goal, structure.

Phrases for steering the meeting

  • Refocus: "Let's bring this back to the main question — [topic]."
  • Park a tangent: "Good point, but it's a separate discussion — let's add it to the parking lot."
  • Move on: "I think we've covered this — let's move to the next item."
  • Bring in someone: "[Name], you've been quiet — what's your take?"
  • Cut a rambler: "Let me jump in — in the interest of time, what's the headline?"
  • Force a decision: "We've been around this for a while. What's our call — A or B?"
  • Time check: "We have 5 minutes left, let's make sure we capture next steps."

Handling the dominator

If one person is talking 80% of the time, intervene:

  • "Thanks [Name]. Let me bring [other person] in — what's your take?"
  • "Hold that thought — [other person] has been wanting to add something."
  • "Quick time check: I want to make sure we hear from everyone."

Handling silence

If you ask a question and silence follows for 5+ seconds, don't fill the silence. Wait. People are thinking. Filling silence trains them to never speak up.

If silence continues for 10 seconds, call on someone gently: "[Name], you've worked on this — any thoughts?"

The recap

The most important 90 seconds of any meeting are the last 90. Always end with:

"OK, to recap: We decided [decision]. [Name] will [action] by [date]. [Name 2] will [action] by [date]. Anything else? Thanks everyone."

The async-first principle

Before scheduling a meeting, ask: could this be an email? Could this be a Slack thread? Could this be a doc with comments?

If the answer is yes, do that instead. The best meeting is one you didn't have to call.

Module 51
Virtual Meetings

Most modern tech meetings are remote or hybrid. The medium changes the rules — what works in a physical conference room often fails on Zoom.

Setup checklist (do once, benefit forever)

  • Camera at eye level. Looking down at a laptop camera shows your nostrils. Stack books or get a stand.
  • Light from in front, not behind. Window or lamp facing you. Backlighting makes you a silhouette.
  • Headset or earbuds. Built-in laptop mic + speaker = echo for everyone else.
  • Wired internet if possible. Wi-fi drops kill credibility.
  • Plain background or a clean virtual background. Avoid the cluttered shelf.
  • Phone on silent. Not vibrate — fully silent.

Camera-on as default

For small meetings (<8 people), keep camera on. Camera-off in a small meeting reads as disengaged or hiding. Camera-off is fine for:

  • Large all-hands (>15 people)
  • Listen-only meetings where you're a peripheral attendee
  • You're walking, eating, or in a non-private space

Speaking on video

  • Look at the camera, not the screen, when speaking. Hard at first; gives the impression of eye contact.
  • Pause longer than feels natural. Audio lag means your "natural" pause feels rushed to others.
  • Announce yourself before speaking in larger meetings: "This is [Name] — quick comment."
  • Don't lean in. Sit comfortably back. Leaning in close looks intense and shows your face too big.
  • Smile. Sounds simple, but on video your default neutral face reads as upset. Slight smile = friendly.

The mute discipline

  • Default: muted
  • Unmute only when speaking
  • Re-mute when done
  • If you forget and your dog barks, just mute — don't apologize at length

Crosstalk handling

Audio lag means two people start speaking at once. The fix:

  • "Sorry, you go first."
  • "After you."
  • (If host) "Let's go [Name 1] first, then [Name 2]."

Don't spend 30 seconds with mutual "no, you go" — pick one and move on.

Reading the room over video

It's harder. You can't see body language well. Compensate by:

  • Asking "any reactions?" or "what do people think?" more often
  • Watching the chat sidebar for written reactions
  • Calling on people by name to surface their views
  • Using polls for binary decisions

Hybrid meetings (some in-room, some remote)

The hardest format. Remote attendees almost always feel left out unless the host actively works to include them.

  • Everyone joins from their own laptop — even people in the same room. Levels the playing field.
  • The host calls on remote people first for input, before in-room people fill the air
  • The agenda + pre-read goes via the same channel for everyone
  • Decisions get written down and shared, not just spoken

Timezone discipline

If you work with international teams, every meeting is a timezone calculation. Best practices:

  • Always include timezone in invites: "10:00 AM IST / 4:30 AM PT"
  • Rotate inconvenience — don't make the same group always do early/late
  • If you're in IST and meeting US: schedule late-evening IST so they're morning PT
  • If you're in IST and meeting Europe: late-afternoon IST is mid-morning CET
  • Use scheduling tools that show all timezones

Recording etiquette

  • If recording, announce it: "I'm going to record this for folks who can't make it."
  • Pause if someone says something they wouldn't say on the record
  • Share recording within 24 hours — old recordings rot
Reality check: the difference between a good remote meeting and a bad one is mostly the host's effort. If you host badly, you waste 10 people's hours. If you host well, you make remote work actually work.

Module 52
Structuring a Presentation

A presentation is not a doc read aloud. It's an argument with a beginning, middle, and end — designed to land one or two ideas firmly in the audience's head. Most engineers structure presentations like a code walkthrough; that's why most engineering presentations bore everyone.

The single most useful question

Before you open slides software: "If the audience remembers ONE thing, what is it?"

That one thing is your headline. Everything else exists to support it.

The classic structure (works for most cases)

  1. Hook (30 seconds) — get attention
  2. Context (1-2 min) — what problem, why it matters now
  3. Body (60% of time) — your 2-4 main points
  4. Recommendation / call to action (1-2 min) — what you want them to do
  5. Q&A (last third)

The hook patterns

  • The surprising number: "Our queue grew from 200 messages a day to 4 million in 18 months."
  • The story: "Last Wednesday at 3am, our payments service started returning 500s — let me tell you what we found."
  • The contrast: "Most teams ship features. The teams that win ship outcomes. Today I'll show you the difference."
  • The question: "If you had to halve our infrastructure cost in a quarter, where would you start?"

Don't open with: "Hi everyone, my name is X, and today I'm going to talk about Y." That's the slide title — say something interesting first.

The body — three patterns

The "rule of three"

Audiences remember 3 things. Maybe 4. They will not remember 7. If you have 7 points, group them into 3.

The closing

The last 30 seconds are the most-remembered. Don't fade out with "and... yeah... that's it." Land the close:

  • "To recap: [the one thing]. The action I'm asking for is [specific ask]. Questions?"
  • "If you take one thing from this: [the headline]. Let's talk about how to make it real."

Time discipline

  • 10-min talk = ~10 slides, not 30. One slide per minute is the upper limit.
  • 30-min talk = leave 10 min for Q&A. So plan 20 min of speaking.
  • 60-min talk = at least 15 min Q&A. Don't speak for 60 minutes straight; you'll lose them.

Always rehearse with a timer. If you go over in rehearsal, cut content — don't try to talk faster.

Module 53
Slide Design

Most presentations have terrible slides. Bullet point walls, microscopic fonts, screenshots that no one can read, cluttered diagrams. Better slides aren't about design talent — they're about restraint.

The five rules

  1. One idea per slide. If your slide has two ideas, split it.
  2. Big text, few words. If a person at the back can't read it, cut it down.
  3. Pictures over paragraphs. A diagram, chart, or photo beats text wherever possible.
  4. The slide is the prompt; you are the content. The slide cues your speaking; it's not your script.
  5. Title that asserts, not labels. "Latency went up 40% after the migration" beats "Latency results."

  • Highlight the relevant series. Color the bar that matters; gray out the others.

  • Label directly. Skip the legend if you can put labels on the data.

Architecture diagrams

For technical presentations, your diagram is the slide. Tips:

  • Build it up step by step across 3-4 slides — don't dump the whole thing at once
  • Color-code: services in one color, data stores in another, external systems in another
  • Show data flow with arrows, not just connection lines
  • Include the "before" and "after" if you're proposing a change

Don't write your full speech in the speaker notes. Write 3-5 bullet points per slide — what you want to remember. Reading speaker notes verbatim sounds robotic.

Code on slides

Avoid if possible. If you must:

  • Maximum 10-15 lines
  • Highlight the 1-2 lines that matter
  • Use a dark theme + monospace font (32pt minimum)
  • Walk through it, don't just paste and read

Real-time coding demos: only do them if you've rehearsed three times. Otherwise have screenshots ready as backup.

Templates and themes

Use your company's slide template if there is one. If not, default to a clean theme with a single accent color. Avoid: ClipArt, transitions, animations beyond simple fade-ins.

Module 54
Delivering Confidently

You've structured a great deck. Now you have to deliver it. Delivery is what separates "OK presentation" from "memorable presentation." This is also where most engineers struggle most.

Before you start

  • Rehearse out loud at least twice. Reading silently doesn't count. Saying the words is a different motor skill.
  • Time yourself. Your in-head pace and your spoken pace differ by 30%.
  • Walk the room (or test the platform) 5 minutes before. Familiar environment = lower anxiety.
  • Open the deck on a backup laptop or USB. Tech failures happen.
  • Pee, drink water, breathe. Mundane but matters.

The first 30 seconds

Your nerves are highest in the first 30 seconds. Do these to settle them:

  1. Pause before you start. Look around for 3 seconds. Sounds eternal; reads as confident.
  2. Speak slower than feels natural. Adrenaline speeds you up — counter it.
  3. Open with something you know cold. Don't improvise the opening — memorize it.

Voice control

  • Volume: louder than you think. People at the back should hear comfortably.
  • Pace: 130-150 words per minute. Slower for technical content. Slower for non-native English audiences.
  • Pitch: end statements going slightly down (.) not up (?). Up-talk makes you sound unsure.
  • Pause: after each main point, 1-2 seconds. Lets the audience absorb. Also: instead of "um."

Body language

  • Stand grounded. Feet shoulder-width, weight even. Don't sway.
  • Open posture. Arms relaxed at sides or gesturing. Not crossed, not in pockets, not behind your back.
  • Move with intent. Move 2-3 steps to make a point. Then plant. Don't pace nervously.
  • Hands above the waist. Gesturing low looks meek; gesturing in the upper body shows energy.
  • Eye contact. Hold each person's eye 2-3 seconds, then move. Don't scan.

On video

  • Look at the camera, not the screen, when delivering key points
  • Smile slightly — your "neutral" face on video reads more serious than in person
  • Lower your hands if they're flying around in front of the camera
  • Keep notes next to the camera, not far below

Killing fillers

Engineers' top fillers: "um," "uh," "like," "basically," "actually," "you know," "right?" Replace them with silence — silence is a filler too, but a confident-sounding one.

Practice exercise

Record yourself giving a 2-minute talk. Listen back. Count the fillers. Now do it again, with a deliberate goal of zero fillers. The first time will feel slow — that's fine. By the third try, it'll feel natural.

If you blank

It happens. Phrases that buy time gracefully:

  • "Let me come back to that in a moment."
  • "What was the question again?"
  • (Pause. Look at slide. Continue.) Silence isn't fatal.
  • "I had a great way of saying this — let me find it again."

If something goes wrong

  • Slide doesn't load: "While that loads, let me tell you about [the next point]."
  • Mic dies: stay calm, gesture for a tech, project your voice if you can
  • Demo breaks: have screenshots ready. "Let me walk you through what this should show."
  • Wrong slide deck: own it, recover. "Apologies — wrong file. Give me one moment." Don't panic.

The audience

Audiences want you to succeed. They came to hear you. They're not waiting to catch you out. The hostile-audience scenario you're imagining is mostly imaginary. Most audiences are tired and distracted; your job is to be interesting, not to defend yourself.

Indian-English presentation patterns

A few patterns common in Indian-English presentations that international audiences perceive differently:

  • Reading from slides: common in Indian academic contexts, but reads as unprepared internationally. Speak around the slide.
  • Excessive deference: "Respected audience..." or "I am most humbly trying to explain..." → just say what you mean.
  • Apologizing pre-emptively: "Sorry if my English is not very good..." → just speak. The apology draws attention to the very thing you don't want highlighted.
  • Saying "thank you" too often during the talk: save it for the end.
  • Stress on every syllable equally: work on stressed-vs-unstressed rhythm (covered in Module 8).

Module 55
Q&A Handling

The questions are sometimes more important than the talk. This is where your real expertise shows. Many people give a great talk and then fall apart in Q&A. The skills are different.

The four-step Q&A pattern

  1. Listen fully — don't start formulating your answer while they're still talking
  2. Acknowledge / repeat — "Great question. So you're asking about [paraphrase]?"
  3. Answer concisely — 30-60 seconds. Don't ramble.
  4. Check — "Does that answer it?" Then move to the next question.

Why repeat the question

  • The audience may not have heard it
  • It buys you 5 seconds to think
  • It confirms you understood
  • It lets the asker correct you if you misheard

Phrases for Q&A

To open Q&A:

  • "Happy to take questions now."
  • "What's on your mind?"
  • "What would you like to dig into?"

To acknowledge:

  • "Great question."
  • "That's a fair point."
  • "Good — let me address that."

If you don't know:

  • "That's a great question — I don't have the data on that. Let me follow up after."
  • "I haven't looked at it from that angle. Let me think about it and get back to you."
  • "Honestly, I don't know. Anyone in the room have insight on this?"

If the question is hostile:

  • "I appreciate the challenge. Here's how I see it: [reasoning]."
  • "Let me make sure I understand the concern: [paraphrase]. Is that fair?"
  • "We don't agree on that, and that's OK. Here's why I think [position]."

If the question is off-topic:

  • "Interesting question — slightly outside the scope today. Let's chat after."
  • "Happy to take that offline."

The "I don't know" rule

If you don't know, say so. Trying to bluff is the single fastest way to lose credibility. "I don't know — let me find out and follow up" is a confident answer.

Hostile or trick questions

  • Stay calm. The audience is watching how you react more than what you say.
  • Don't get defensive. Defensive responses look like you have something to hide.
  • Don't argue. "I see your point. Here's where I disagree: ..." is fine. "You're wrong" is not.
  • Don't ramble to fill time. Short, confident answers beat long, anxious ones.

The compound question

Sometimes you'll get "Three questions: A, B, and C." Address them one at a time:

"Three good questions. Let me take them in order. On A: [answer]. On B: [answer]. C is the most interesting — [answer]."

The statement-disguised-as-a-question

Someone takes 90 seconds to make their own point and ends with "...so what do you think?" Acknowledge their point, give a brief response, move on:

"Thanks for that perspective. Where I'd add to it is [your point]. Next question?"

When to take questions

  • End of talk: standard, lets you control the narrative
  • Throughout: works for small audiences (<15) or workshops
  • Mix: "Quick clarifying questions as we go, deeper questions at the end"

State which one at the start: "I'll take questions at the end" or "Feel free to interrupt."

Closing Q&A

Don't let Q&A peter out. Close it with energy:

"Last question? OK — thanks everyone. The deck and notes will go out by tomorrow. I'll be around for offline questions."

Owning the closing prevents the awkward "is it... over?" moment.

Part L — Difficult Conversations

Module 56
Conflict Resolution

Conflict at work is inevitable. The myth is that healthy teams have no conflict. Healthy teams have conflict — they just handle it well. The unhealthy pattern is conflict avoided until it festers, then explodes.

What conflict actually is

A conflict is a difference in views, priorities, or expectations between two people. It's not the same as personal hostility. Most conflicts are about:

  • Process — how something should be done
  • Priority — what should be done first
  • Resources — who gets what
  • Recognition — who gets credit
  • Information gap — one person knows something the other doesn't

The escalation ladder

  1. Direct conversation with the person — start here
  2. Written follow-up if direct conversation didn't resolve
  3. Bring in your manager / their manager as a second pair of eyes
  4. Formal escalation (HR, conflict resolution process) — last resort

Most conflicts should resolve at step 1 or 2. Going straight to step 3 makes everyone defensive and damages relationships.

The 1:1 conversation framework

When you sit down with the person:

  1. Open with intent. "I want to clear the air about [thing]. I value working with you and want to find a way through."
  2. State your view factually. "From where I'm sitting, [observation]. [Impact on you]."
  3. Listen to theirs. "Help me understand how you see it."
  4. Find the actual disagreement. Often you agree on more than you thought.
  5. Agree a path forward. "What if we did X going forward?"

Phrases to de-escalate

  • "I think we got off on the wrong foot — can we reset?"
  • "I want to make sure I'm understanding you correctly."
  • "Let me share where I'm coming from — and I want to hear your side too."
  • "I might be wrong about this. Help me see it your way."
  • "What would you need from me to move past this?"
  • "What can we agree on?"

Phrases to avoid

  • "You always..." / "You never..." → almost always inaccurate; triggers defense
  • "Calm down" → guarantees the opposite
  • "That's ridiculous" / "That's wrong" → shuts down dialogue
  • "You're being [emotion]" → diagnosing emotions is patronizing
  • "I'm just being honest" → cover for being harsh

The "I" vs "You" rule

Frame issues from your experience, not as accusations:

Accusatory ("you")

  • "You ignored my comments on the PR."
  • "You took credit for my work."
  • "You're not pulling your weight."

Observational ("I")

  • "I noticed my comments weren't addressed in the PR — wanted to understand why."
  • "In the meeting today, I felt my contribution wasn't credited. I'd like to talk about it."
  • "I've been noticing the workload split feels uneven — can we look at it together?"

When the other person is angry

  • Don't match their energy. Stay calm; their volume drops to meet yours.
  • Listen. Often anger is just unmet need to feel heard.
  • Acknowledge feelings without agreeing with conclusions. "I can see this has been frustrating" doesn't mean "you're right."
  • If it's escalating, pause. "Let's take 30 minutes and come back to this."

When you're the angry one

  • Sleep on it before responding
  • Write the angry email; save as draft; revisit in 4 hours
  • Walk before the conversation — physical movement helps
  • Have the conversation with someone else first (manager, friend) to dump emotion before the actual one

The relationship over the issue

Most issues are short-term. Most working relationships are long-term. If you "win" an argument but damage the relationship, you usually lose. Optimize for working together six months from now, not for being right today.

Module 57
Apologizing

Knowing how to apologize well is rare and underrated. Most people either over-apologize ("Sorry, sorry, sorry!") or apologize so vaguely it doesn't help. A clear apology rebuilds trust faster than perfection.

The four parts of a real apology

  1. Specific acknowledgment of what you did
  2. Acknowledgment of impact on the other person
  3. What you'll do differently going forward
  4. (Optional) make it right — if there's a fix possible

Real vs fake apologies

Fake apologies

  • "Sorry if you were upset." (= "your reaction is the problem")
  • "Sorry, but..." (= "I'm not actually sorry")
  • "Sorry you feel that way." (= "your feelings are wrong")
  • "Mistakes were made." (= passive, no ownership)
  • "I'm sorry you took it the wrong way." (= "you misunderstood")
  • "Sorry, I'm just like that." (= "deal with it")

Real apologies

  • "I missed the deadline. That put pressure on the team. I should have flagged earlier — going forward I'll give a heads up by Wednesday if a Friday is at risk."
  • "I interrupted you in the meeting. That wasn't fair, and I want to make sure your point gets heard. I'll make space for you to finish today."
  • "I sent that Slack message in frustration. I'm sorry — it wasn't a useful way to raise the concern. Can we talk through it properly?"

The "but" trap

"I'm sorry I missed the deadline, BUT the requirements weren't clear" — the "but" cancels the apology. The other person hears: "you're blaming me." If you have context, give it separately, after the apology lands:

"I missed the deadline. That's on me — I should have communicated sooner. [pause] For context, the requirements shifted twice mid-sprint, which is part of why I want to talk about how we capture changes going forward."

Don't over-apologize

Some Indian-English speakers apologize 5 times for one thing. International readers find it weakening — it suggests guilt rather than accountability. Apologize once, well. Then move forward.

Over-apologizing

"Sorry, so sorry, I really am very sorry, I apologize sincerely, I should never have done it, I'm so so sorry, please forgive me, I won't ever do it again, sorry sorry sorry."

Once, well

"I'm sorry — that was my mistake. Here's what I'll do differently next time."

Apologies in writing

For meaningful apologies (missed deadlines, broken promises, mistakes affecting others), writing is often better than speaking — it gives you time to be precise.

"Hi [Name],

I want to apologize for missing our 2pm call yesterday. I dropped the ball on putting it on my calendar after we shifted it Tuesday. That meant you had to reschedule your afternoon, and the scoping discussion got delayed.

Going forward, I'll add meetings to my calendar in real-time during our conversations rather than relying on memory. Happy to do the scoping at any time today or tomorrow that works for you.

Sorry again,
[Name]"

When the apology isn't accepted

Sometimes you apologize and the other person isn't ready to move on. That's their right. Don't push:

  • "I understand. Take whatever time you need."
  • "I get it. I'm here when you want to talk further."

Don't keep apologizing — that becomes pressure. One sincere apology is yours to give. Acceptance is theirs to give in their time.

When you're the one being apologized to

  • "Thanks for saying that. I appreciate it."
  • "I appreciate you owning it. Let's move forward."
  • "Thank you. That means something. Here's what would help me..."

If you're not ready to fully forgive, be honest: "Thanks. I need a bit of time to process — but I appreciate you saying it."

Module 58
Negotiating

You negotiate more often than you realize: salary, scope, deadlines, ownership of features, who runs the meeting, who takes the on-call shift. The skill isn't aggression — it's preparation, framing, and patience.

The mindset shift

Indian-English culture often frames negotiation as confrontational or impolite. International business culture treats it as routine and expected. Asking for more (with reason) is professional, not rude. Not asking is leaving value on the table.

The 4-step preparation

  1. Know what you want. Specific number, deadline, or outcome.
  2. Know your floor. The minimum you'll accept (your "BATNA" — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement).
  3. Know their incentives. What does the other side care about?
  4. Have data. Market rates, comparable timelines, examples — facts beat opinions.

The opening move

Whoever names a number first sets the anchor. Anchor high (when you want more) or low (when offering less). The middle is closer to the anchor than the other side's number.

  • If you want $130K and you think they'll offer $110K, ask for $140-150K, not $135K. The negotiation usually meets in the middle.
  • If you're committing to a deadline, give yourself buffer: ask for 4 weeks for a 3-week task. You can finish early; you can't ask for more time later without consequences.

Salary negotiation phrases

  • "Based on my research and the scope of the role, I was looking at [number]."
  • "Could we look at [number]? Here's why: [data, market rates, your experience]."
  • "I'm excited about the role. Is there flexibility on the offer?"
  • "What would it take to get to [number]?"
  • "If the base isn't flexible, can we look at [signing bonus / equity / earlier review / extra PTO]?"
  • "Can I have a couple of days to consider?"

Scope / deadline negotiation

For internal negotiations (PM wants more features, manager wants tighter deadline):

  • "I can do A and B by Friday. C will need to slip to next sprint. Which matters more?"
  • "To hit Friday, we'd need to cut [feature]. Are you OK with that trade?"
  • "I can hit the deadline OR I can keep quality. Pick one."
  • "If we add this scope, the timeline moves. Want me to draft the new timeline?"
  • "Let me come back with a proposal that meets the constraint."

The silence technique

State your number or position. Then stop talking. Most people fill silence by negotiating against themselves — "...but I could do less if that's not workable" — before the other side has even responded.

Silence after a number forces the other side to respond. Whoever speaks first usually gives ground.

Don't give without getting

When the other side asks for a concession, attach a counter-ask:

  • "I can do that — if we can also [your ask]."
  • "Yes to X, but in exchange I need Y."

Free concessions teach the other side that asking gets them more for nothing.

Walking away

The most powerful position in any negotiation is genuine willingness to walk. If you can't walk, you can't really negotiate. Phrases:

  • "If we can't get to [number/term], it's not the right fit for me."
  • "I appreciate the offer. I think we're too far apart on this — let me know if anything changes."
  • "Let me think about it. I'll get back to you tomorrow."

Don't bluff — empty walks ruin trust. Only walk if you actually mean it.

What NOT to do

  • Don't accept on the spot — even good offers benefit from "let me think about it"
  • Don't apologize for negotiating — "Sorry to ask but..." undermines you
  • Don't share your floor. "I'd take anything above $X" guarantees you get $X.
  • Don't use ultimatums lightly. "If you don't give me X, I quit" only works if you're actually leaving.
  • Don't lie about competing offers. If discovered, you've burned the relationship permanently.

After the negotiation

Whether you got what you wanted or not — be gracious. Today's "no" might be next quarter's "yes." Burn no bridges.

"Thanks for working through this with me. I appreciate the conversation."

Module 59
Confronting Issues

Sometimes you have to raise an uncomfortable issue — with a peer, a manager, a report, a stakeholder. Avoiding it doesn't make it go away; it usually makes it bigger. Confronting issues well is a senior-level skill.

When to confront

  • The same issue has come up 2-3 times
  • The issue affects work outcomes, not just preferences
  • You can't move forward without addressing it
  • Avoiding it is starting to affect you (resentment, stress, performance)

When NOT to confront

  • It's a one-off and unlikely to repeat
  • You're emotionally activated — wait until you're calm
  • You don't have the data to support what you're saying
  • The relationship can't sustain it (extreme cases — usually it can)

The SBI framework (Situation-Behavior-Impact)

  1. Situation — when and where it happened
  2. Behavior — what specifically the person did (observable, not interpretation)
  3. Impact — what effect it had on you / the team / the work

Then leave space for their response. SBI keeps you on facts, not character attacks.

Example: "In yesterday's planning meeting (situation), when I started explaining the API design, you cut in twice before I finished (behavior). It made it hard to lay out the proposal, and I'm not sure people heard the full picture (impact). What was going on for you?"

Confronting a peer

Peer-to-peer is the most common case. Tips:

  • Do it in private — never in front of others if you can help it
  • Prefer in-person or video over text — tone matters
  • Open with intent: "I want to raise something — not a big deal, but worth talking through."
  • Use SBI
  • Be open to learning you misread the situation

Confronting your manager

Harder, especially for Indian-English speakers where deference is cultural. But managers need direct feedback to lead well. Phrases:

  • "There's something I want to raise — is now a good time?"
  • "I want to give you some feedback. I appreciate working with you, and I want to make sure we keep our communication good."
  • "In [meeting / interaction], I felt [reaction]. I wanted to flag it because [why it matters]."
  • "What's your perspective?"

Most managers welcome this — if yours doesn't, that's important data about whether to stay.

Confronting a direct report

If you manage people, you'll need to raise performance, behavior, or pattern issues. Tips:

  • Earlier is easier — issues addressed at "small problem" stage rarely become "big problem"
  • Be specific. "You're underperforming" is useless. "You missed three of the last four sprint commitments — let's talk about why" is actionable.
  • Listen as much as you talk
  • End with clear expectations: "Going forward, I need to see X by [date]"
  • Document — for serious issues, follow up in writing

Confronting a stakeholder / customer

Often someone outside your team is creating problems — unrealistic expectations, scope creep, blocking decisions. Phrases:

  • "I want to raise a concern about how this is going."
  • "From my side, [observation]. That's creating [problem]. How can we work this differently?"
  • "I think we need to recalibrate. Here's what I'm seeing — what are you seeing?"
  • "This isn't sustainable. Let's talk about how we adjust."

Pre-conversation prep

Before any hard conversation:

  1. Write down the facts (specific examples with dates if possible)
  2. Write down what you want as an outcome
  3. Anticipate their response — what might they say? How will you reply?
  4. Practice opening sentence aloud — once is enough

If it goes badly

Sometimes the conversation doesn't land. The other person gets defensive, denies, or attacks back. You can:

  • Pause: "Let me think about what you've said. Can we come back to this?"
  • Acknowledge difference: "We see this differently. Let's at least agree on [smaller point]."
  • Escalate: bring in a manager / mediator
  • Document and move on: not every confrontation resolves on the first try

The follow-up

After a hard conversation, send a brief written summary:

"Thanks for the chat. To recap what we agreed: [points]. Let's check in [next week / in the next 1:1]. Appreciate you talking it through."

Written follow-up makes the conversation real and reduces "wait, did we agree?" later.

The reframe: a confrontation isn't an attack. It's an act of respect. By raising the issue, you're saying "I value this enough to do the hard work of fixing it, rather than letting it rot." Most people, given time, recognize that.

Module 60
Slack & Teams

Slack and Teams are the dominant medium for engineering communication today. The norms differ from email in important ways — getting them right shapes how people perceive you on a daily basis.

The "Hi, can I ask a question?" anti-pattern

Bad: hello first

10:14 — "Hi"
10:14 — (you wait, see "typing...")
10:15 — "Are you free?"
10:15 — (you reply: "what's up?")
10:16 — "I have a question"
10:17 — (you reply: "go ahead")
10:18 — "Could you check the deployment status?"

Five minutes wasted. They could have asked the question at 10:14.

Good: ask the actual question

10:14 — "Hey — could you check the deployment status when you have a sec? Want to confirm before I update the team."

Don't say hi. Just ask. See nohello.net — the cultural norm in international tech.

Threading discipline

  • Reply in threads when you have something specific to add to a topic
  • Don't break the thread by replying in the main channel — keeps the channel clean
  • "Also send to channel" only when the response should be visible to everyone, not just thread participants
  • Start new threads for new topics — don't pile six different questions in one thread

Channel etiquette

  • @here — pings online members. Use rarely (1-2x a week max in a busy channel)
  • @channel — pings everyone. Reserve for important announcements only
  • @everyone — pings the entire workspace. Almost never appropriate
  • @username — direct ping. The default for getting one person's attention

Over-pinging trains people to mute you. Each ping should be worth the interrupt.

Async timezone respect

If you work with international teams: don't expect immediate replies. Frame messages with that in mind:

  • "Hey — when you're online tomorrow, could you take a look at..."
  • "No rush — got this when you can."
  • "Quick question for whenever — the answer will help me unblock the deploy in your morning."

Tone in chat

Chat compresses tone. Things that read fine spoken can read sharp written. Counter with:

  • Soft openers: "Hey", "Just a quick one"
  • Acknowledgments: "Thanks!", "Got it", "Makes sense"
  • Emojis: appropriate for the team culture; ✅ for confirmation, 🙏 for thanks, 👀 for "I'm looking"
  • One exclamation point sparingly; not five

Reading too much into responses

"OK." with a period from someone you barely know feels cold but usually means "OK." People are busy. Don't spiral over single-character replies.

Status messages

Setting your status helps the team:

  • 🍔 Lunch — back at 1:30
  • 🎯 Heads down — reply tomorrow
  • 🤒 Sick — back tomorrow
  • 🌴 OOO until June 5
  • 📞 In meetings until 4pm

DM vs channel

  • If the answer would help others, post in channel
  • If it's personal/private, DM
  • If you're unsure, ask: "Should I take this to the channel?"
  • Avoid DMing one person to bypass team visibility — looks like end-running

Reactions instead of replies

For acknowledgments, reactions beat replies:

  • 👍 = "got it" / "agreed"
  • ✅ = "done"
  • 👀 = "I'm looking at this"
  • 🙏 = "thanks"
  • ❤️ = "appreciate this" (warmer than 👍)

Saves notification noise. Twenty "thanks!" replies in a thread is annoying; twenty 🙏 reactions is fine.

What NOT to do

  • Don't post sensitive info (passwords, tokens, customer data) — even in DMs
  • Don't gossip about colleagues in channels
  • Don't post screenshots that show other people's private info
  • Don't auto-paste long stack traces — use a thread or paste/snippet feature
  • Don't post "any update?" 30 minutes after a question — give people hours
  • Don't ping at 11pm "to ask a quick question" — schedule the message for next morning

Module 61
Documentation Writing

Code comments lie. Slack threads disappear. Meeting minutes get lost. Documentation is what survives. Engineers who write good docs become disproportionately influential — their work scales beyond themselves.

The audience question

Before writing any doc, ask: who is this for?

  • Future you (6 months from now) — context you'll forget
  • New teammates — onboarding material
  • Other teams — what your service does and why
  • External users / customers — how to use your API/product
  • Decision-makers — proposals, ADRs, RFCs

Each audience needs different depth, jargon, and structure.

The doc types you'll write most

Doc typePurposeLength
READMEWhat is this, how to run it1-2 pages
RFC / Design docPropose an approach3-10 pages
ADRRecord an architecture decision1 page
RunbookHow to fix a known issue1 page per scenario
PostmortemWhat happened, why, what changes2-4 pages
API docsHow to call the endpointsVaries
Onboarding docHow a new engineer gets productive5-15 pages

The universal structure

  1. Title — what is this
  2. TL;DR / Summary — 2-3 sentences
  3. Context / motivation — why this exists
  4. Body — the actual content
  5. Open questions / next steps — what's unresolved

The TL;DR matters most. Half the readers will read only that.

Writing style for docs

  • Active voice. "The service handles requests" — not "Requests are handled by the service."
  • Short sentences. 15-20 words is plenty. If you can split it, split it.
  • Concrete examples. Show, don't just tell. Code samples, screenshots, real data.
  • Headers and bullets over walls of prose. Readers scan first, read second.
  • One concept per paragraph. Multi-concept paragraphs get re-read.

The README pattern

A good README has these sections:

  1. What this is (1-2 sentences)
  2. Why it exists (1-2 sentences)
  3. How to install / run (commands)
  4. How to test
  5. Common tasks (with examples)
  6. Architecture overview (diagram if possible)
  7. Owner / who to ask
  8. Related links

The RFC / design doc pattern

  1. Title: "RFC: Use Kafka for inter-service notifications"
  2. Author + date
  3. TL;DR: 3 sentences — what, why, recommendation
  4. Background: what's the current state, what's the problem
  5. Goals + non-goals: what this solves, what it doesn't
  6. Proposed approach: the design
  7. Alternatives considered: what else you looked at, why you didn't pick it
  8. Trade-offs / risks: honest about downsides
  9. Rollout plan: how you ship this
  10. Open questions: what's unresolved

The ADR (Architecture Decision Record)

One page, one decision, written when the decision is made.

# ADR-024: Use PostgreSQL JSON columns for audit metadata

**Status:** Accepted (2026-01-15)
**Decision:** Store audit metadata in JSONB columns rather than a separate
  schema-per-tenant approach.
**Context:** We have 5,000+ tenants, each with custom audit fields. A separate
  schema per tenant doesn't scale operationally.
**Consequences:**
  + Flexible — tenants can add fields without migrations
  + Operational simplicity — one schema to manage
  - Less type safety — fields aren't validated at the DB level
  - Query performance is slower for some access patterns
**Alternatives considered:** Schema-per-tenant (ruled out for ops cost).
  EAV pattern (ruled out for query complexity).

The postmortem pattern

  1. Summary: what broke, when, who was affected
  2. Timeline: minute-by-minute (in UTC) of what happened
  3. Root cause: the underlying reason
  4. Detection: how we noticed (and how we should have noticed faster)
  5. Resolution: what fixed it
  6. Action items: what we'll do to prevent recurrence (with owners + dates)
  7. Lessons learned

Postmortems should be blameless — focus on the system, not the individual. "The deploy script didn't have a confirmation step" — not "Bob hit yes too fast."

What NOT to write

  • Anything that says "TBD" forever — either fill it in or remove it
  • Long historical context that doesn't help the reader (no one cares about Q3 2022 unless it directly motivates today's design)
  • Comments inside the doc like "Bob said this is wrong but I disagree"
  • Anything that contradicts another doc — link instead, single source of truth
  • Marketing fluff — "best-in-class," "synergistic," "leveraging" — say what it does

Doc maintenance

  • Date your docs (last updated)
  • Mark obsolete docs clearly: "DEPRECATED — see [new doc]"
  • Review docs quarterly for important systems — drift happens
  • Delete docs that aren't true anymore. A wrong doc is worse than no doc.

Module 62
Pull Requests & Code Review

Code reviews are pure communication. The code is the artifact; the review is the conversation. Done well, reviews catch bugs, spread knowledge, and build trust. Done badly, they breed resentment and slow the team down.

Writing a good PR description

The PR description is the frame for the entire review. Sloppy descriptions get sloppy reviews.

Template:

  • What: 1-2 sentences on what this PR does
  • Why: the problem this solves (link to ticket if there is one)
  • How: brief notes on the approach, especially any non-obvious choices
  • Testing: what tests exist, what you ran, screenshots if UI
  • Trade-offs / known limitations: what's not in scope
  • Reviewer notes: "Please pay attention to X" or "Y is intentional"

The PR size rule

  • Under 200 lines: easy to review, gets thorough attention
  • 200-500 lines: review quality drops; reviewers skim
  • Over 500 lines: usually rubber-stamped — review value evaporates

If your PR is huge, split it. Stacked PRs (PR 2 builds on PR 1) is the standard solution.

Writing review comments

Reviewers and authors are partners, not adversaries. Tone matters. The same critique can land like collaboration or like attack:

Harsh / accusatory

  • "This is wrong."
  • "Why did you do it this way?"
  • "You forgot the null check."
  • "This will obviously break in prod."
  • "Did you even test this?"

Collaborative

  • "I think there's an edge case here when X — what do you think?"
  • "Curious about the choice to do it this way over [alternative] — what's the reasoning?"
  • "Looks like this might fail when input is null — should we add a check?"
  • "I'm worried this could break in prod under [scenario] — could we add a test?"
  • "Did this scenario get tested? I might have missed it in the test file."

Comment severity prefixes

A useful convention to make review intent clear:

  • nit: small style issue, optional ("nit: extra blank line here")
  • question: not asking for a change, just curious ("question: why this approach?")
  • suggestion: proposing a different approach, take it or leave it
  • blocker: must fix before merging
  • praise: "+1, this is clean!" — good feedback isn't only critical

Phrases that work for reviewers

  • "Have we considered..."
  • "What about the case where..."
  • "This is interesting — could you walk me through the reasoning?"
  • "Just to make sure I follow..."
  • "In a previous project we hit this — wondering if it applies."
  • "Optional, but: [suggestion]"
  • "Nit, take or leave: [tiny thing]"
  • "Approving — left a couple of comments to address before merge."
  • "Approving with one ask: [thing]."

Receiving review feedback

Reviews can sting. The first instinct is to defend. Resist it. The reviewer is helping.

  • Read all comments before replying. Don't reply emotionally to the first sharp one.
  • Default to "you're right." Most review feedback is good. Save defenses for cases that genuinely matter.
  • If you disagree, explain calmly. "I considered that — here's why I went with this approach: [reasoning]. Open to changing if you still see issues."
  • Thank the reviewer. Especially for thorough reviews. They spent their time on you.

Phrases that work for authors responding

  • "Good catch, fixed."
  • "Fixed in [next commit]."
  • "Thanks — addressed."
  • "Fair point. Let me think about it." (then act)
  • "I considered that. Here's why I went with this — [reason]. Want to discuss?"
  • "You're right, I missed that. Updated."
  • "That's outside scope — opened ticket #X to track."
  • "Thanks for the thorough review!"

When the reviewer is wrong

Sometimes the reviewer is mistaken. The right response isn't to silently make the change — it's to push back politely:

"I see what you mean, but I don't think we want to change this — here's why: [reason]. The current behavior is intentional because [context]. Happy to discuss if you still think it should change."

Pushing back politely is healthy. Caving on every review comment makes the codebase incoherent.

Async review pacing

  • If a PR sits over 24 hours without review, it's reasonable to nudge once
  • If you've been blocked for 3+ days, escalate ("Hey, I've been blocked on this since Monday — could anyone take a look?")
  • Reviewers should aim to respond within 24 hours, even if just "I'll get to it tomorrow"

The "Approve with comments" pattern

If feedback is mostly nits and the change is good overall, approve and leave nits inline:

"Approving — looks good overall. Left a few nits you can take or leave. Feel free to merge after addressing the [specific thing]."

This unblocks the author rather than forcing a re-review for trivial changes.

The "Request changes" pattern

Reserve "Request changes" for issues that genuinely need fixing before merge: bugs, security issues, big design problems. Using it for nits is heavy-handed and slows the team.

Module 63
Interview English

Tech interviews — especially with international companies — happen entirely in English under time pressure. Many qualified Indian engineers underperform interviews not on technical skills but on how they explain their thinking. The good news: this is learnable.

The behavioral interview

Most international tech interviews include behavioral questions: "Tell me about a time when..." The expected answer follows the STAR structure:

  1. Situation — context (1-2 sentences)
  2. Task — what you needed to do (1 sentence)
  3. Action — what YOU did (3-5 sentences)
  4. Result — what happened, with numbers if possible (1-2 sentences)

STAR in practice

"Tell me about a time you handled a production issue."

"In my last role, our payments service started returning 500s on Black Friday — about 20% of transactions failing (S). I was the on-call engineer and needed to mitigate fast (T). I rolled back the most recent deploy as a first step, which dropped the error rate by half. Then I checked the logs and found a connection pool exhaustion — I increased the pool size and pushed a hotfix. I also added an alert for pool saturation so we'd catch it next time (A). Errors went back to baseline within 40 minutes; we lost about $50K in transactions during the window. The post-mortem became a runbook other teams use now (R)."

Common behavioral questions to prepare

  • "Tell me about yourself." (60-90 seconds, work-focused)
  • "Tell me about a project you're proud of."
  • "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager."
  • "Tell me about a time you failed."
  • "Tell me about a time you had to learn something new quickly."
  • "Tell me about a conflict with a teammate."
  • "Why are you leaving your current job?"
  • "Why do you want to work here?"

The "Tell me about yourself" answer

Don't recite your resume. Instead:

  1. Where you are now (1 sentence)
  2. How you got there (2-3 sentences — the relevant journey)
  3. What you're looking for (1 sentence — leads into "why this job")

Technical interviews — talking while you think

Interviewers can't read your mind. If you go silent for 5 minutes, they assume you're stuck. Narrate your thinking:

  • "Let me make sure I understand the problem first..."
  • "I'm thinking about two approaches — one with X, one with Y."
  • "Let me try the simpler one first and optimize if needed."
  • "The trade-off here is between [A] and [B]. I'd lean toward [A] because..."
  • "I'm not sure about the time complexity yet — let me think through it."
  • "That edge case is interesting — let me handle it."

When you're stuck

Don't pretend, don't panic. Interviewers value problem-solving more than instant answers.

  • "I'm stuck on this — can I think out loud?"
  • "Let me try a smaller version of the problem first."
  • "Could you give me a hint on whether I'm on the right track?"
  • "Let me back up — I think I went the wrong direction at [step]."

Questions YOU should ask

End-of-interview "any questions?" is not a formality. Asking nothing reads as disinterested. Good questions:

  • "What does success look like in this role at 6 months?"
  • "What's the biggest challenge facing the team right now?"
  • "How does the team make architectural decisions?"
  • "What's the on-call expectation?"
  • "What did the last person in this role do well or struggle with?"
  • "What's the team's view on technical debt?"

Salary expectations question

"What are your expectations?" is a negotiation trap. Possible answers:

  • "I'd love to learn more about the role and what the band looks like — could you share that?"
  • "My research suggests this role pays in the [range] band — does that align?"
  • "I'm flexible on base depending on the total package. What's the budget for the role?"

Try not to name a specific number first.

Phrases for the closing

  • "Thanks for the time today — really enjoyed the conversation."
  • "This sounds like a great fit. What's the next step?"
  • "Looking forward to hearing back."

The follow-up email

Send within 24 hours of an interview:

"Hi [Name], thanks for the conversation today. I enjoyed digging into [specific topic discussed]. Excited about the team and the role. Happy to share any additional info that would help. Looking forward to hearing about next steps. — [Your name]"

Indian-English patterns to watch in interviews

  • Don't say "Sir" repeatedly. First name once they've introduced themselves.
  • Don't open with "Sorry, my English is not very good" — apology you don't need.
  • Stress and rhythm matter: see Module 8.
  • Don't head-bobble for "yes" on video — use a clear nod or say "yes" / "got it."
  • Don't use overly formal phrases: "I have done my graduation in Computer Science from..." → "I studied Computer Science at..."

Module 64
Networking

Networking is uncomfortable for most engineers, especially in international/remote contexts. The good news: most people you'd want to network with are also engineers, also a bit awkward, and also genuinely interested in what you do. It's less performative than it sounds.

Why networking matters

  • Most jobs aren't found on job boards — they're found through people who know you
  • Career advice from someone 3 steps ahead is more useful than any blog post
  • You learn about industries and roles that you'd never have found alone
  • Help becomes mutual: people you help today help you in 5 years

The reframe

Networking isn't selling yourself. It's building relationships with people who share your interests. The asks come later, after the relationship exists.

Cold outreach (LinkedIn / email)

Reaching out to a stranger is the highest-leverage networking move. Most people won't reply; some will. Format:

"Hi [Name],

I came across your post on [specific thing] — your point about [specific thing] really resonated, especially [your take on it].

I'm a [your role] working on [adjacent thing]. Would love to ask you a couple of questions about how you approached [specific area] when you have 15 minutes. Happy to work around your schedule.

Either way, thanks for the public writing — it's been useful.

[Your name]"

Notice: specific reference to their work, specific ask, easy out, no pressure.

What NOT to write in cold outreach

  • "Hi, can we connect?" — empty
  • "I'm looking for a job at your company" — too direct, no relationship
  • "I'd love to pick your brain" — overused phrase, sounds parasitic
  • Generic greeting with no reference to who they are
  • Long autobiography — they didn't ask
  • Asking for referral immediately — referral is what you build toward

The 15-minute coffee chat

If they say yes, respect their time:

  1. Be on time, prepared. Have specific questions ready.
  2. Open with a thanks + recap your context (30 seconds).
  3. Ask, don't pitch. Listen 70% of the time.
  4. Specific questions: "How did you transition from X to Y?" beats "What's your advice?"
  5. End on time even if they say "we have more time" — they have other things to do.
  6. Send a thank-you within 24 hours.
  7. Follow up 3-6 months later with what you did with their advice.

Conferences and events

Conferences are still where strong relationships start. Tips for engineers who find them awkward:

  • Talk to the speakers. They expect to be approached after their talk. Open with: "Loved your talk — the part about X stuck with me."
  • Look for small clusters at lunch tables. Bigger groups are harder to enter; 2-3 people are easy.
  • Have a 30-second answer to "what do you do?" Practice it.
  • Ask, then listen. "What brought you here?" or "What's been the most useful talk so far?"
  • Exchange contact info before walking away, not after.
  • It's OK to leave a conversation: "Great talking to you — I'm going to grab a coffee, hopefully we'll cross paths later."

Online communities

Discord servers, Slack communities, GitHub projects — modern networking happens here as much as in person:

  • Lurk first to learn the culture
  • Help others before asking for help (answer questions, share resources)
  • Show your work publicly (blog, GitHub, talks) — gives people reason to engage
  • DMs to people whose work you appreciate, with specific reference to that work

Maintaining the network

The hard part isn't meeting people; it's staying in touch.

  • Quarterly: send a short note to 3-5 people you haven't talked to lately
  • Annually: lunch / coffee with each meaningful contact, even briefly
  • When something good happens (promotion, job change, big project), share it with your network
  • Send articles or resources you think they'd care about — no agenda

The phrase to remember

"Just thinking of you — wanted to share this." Send a relevant article or thought to someone in your network with no ask. Builds relationships invisibly.

Asking for a referral

Eventually you may want a referral. Earn it by having a relationship first. Then:

"Hi [Name],

I noticed [Company] is hiring for [specific role]. I'm seriously considering applying — the [specific aspect of the role] really fits where I want to grow.

Would you be open to referring me? Totally understand if it's not a fit — I know referrals are a vouching, and I'd want you to feel comfortable. Happy to send my resume / a write-up of why I'm interested if helpful.

Either way, hope you're doing well.

[Your name]"

Make it easy to say yes and easy to say no. The "no pressure" framing protects the relationship if they decline.

Module 65
Public Speaking

Public speaking is the highest-leverage communication skill in any career. People who can stand up and explain their ideas to a room get promoted, get listened to, and shape the team's direction. It's also one of the most common fears.

The myth: "you have to be a natural"

You don't. Almost every confident speaker started awful. Public speaking is mostly practice — five talks gets you decent, twenty gets you good, fifty gets you compelling.

Levels of public speaking

LevelFormatAudience size
Internal teamStand-up demo, design walk-through5-15
Cross-teamRFC discussion, all-hands update20-100
Company-wideTown hall, big launch100+
ExternalConference talk, podcast, webinar50-1000s

Build up the ladder. Don't volunteer for a 500-person keynote as your first talk.

The fear and what to do about it

The fear of public speaking is universal. Top mitigations:

  1. Be prepared. Most fear comes from "what if I forget what to say?" Preparation removes 80% of the fear.
  2. Rehearse out loud. Three times minimum. The fear shrinks each time.
  3. Visualize success. Imagine yourself walking off having done well. Sounds woo, works.
  4. Reframe nerves as energy. Heart racing isn't "I'm scared" — it's "my body is preparing me."
  5. Pre-talk physical routine: walk, deep breaths, water, no caffeine in the last hour.

Building stage presence

  • Plant your feet. Don't sway. Anchor yourself.
  • Pause more than feels natural. Pauses make the audience pay attention.
  • Project your voice. Loud enough that the back row is comfortable, not straining.
  • Make deliberate eye contact with one person at a time. Hold for 3 seconds, move.
  • Use space. Move 2-3 steps to make a point. Stand still otherwise.
  • Smile at the start. It signals you're glad to be there. The audience relaxes.

Storytelling beats explanation

Audiences remember stories, not bullet points. Even technical talks should have stories woven in:

  • The story of the customer that led to the feature
  • The story of the outage that motivated the design
  • The story of the trade-off you wrestled with

"Here's how we built our caching layer" is information. "Last year we got a midnight page because our dashboard was 11 seconds slow — here's what we learned" is a story.

The conference talk arc

  1. Hook — story, surprising stat, provocative question
  2. Promise — what the audience will leave knowing
  3. Body — 2-3 main points, each with story + explanation + takeaway
  4. Recap — restate the points
  5. Call to action — what do you want them to do/think differently

Tools to improve fast

  • Toastmasters — local clubs, structured speaking practice. Cheap and effective.
  • Record yourself. Watch back. Painful but accelerates improvement.
  • Watch great speakers. TED talks, conference keynotes — note what they do (pacing, gestures, opens).
  • Volunteer for low-stakes talks. Internal lunch-and-learns, hackathon presentations.
  • Get feedback. Ask 1-2 trusted people to honestly tell you what was good and what to improve.

Indian-English speakers — common improvement areas

  • Stress patterns: equal stress on every syllable makes you sound unenergetic. Work on stressing key words (Module 8).
  • Pace: often too fast under nerves. Practice slowing down.
  • "Same to same," "do the needful," "kindly": avoid in international audiences.
  • Head movements: the Indian head-bobble can confuse — for international audiences, default to clear nods or no nod.
  • "Yes Sir/Madam" patterns from school don't translate to corporate stages.

Module 66
Working with International Teams

Most Indian engineers in tech today work with international teams — US, Europe, Australia, Singapore. Each has its own communication norms. Understanding the differences saves a lot of friction.

The core skill: cultural calibration

Different cultures have different defaults for:

  • Directness — how much you say vs leave implied
  • Hierarchy — how much deference is expected
  • Context — high-context (much implied) vs low-context (everything explicit)
  • Time — strict vs flexible relationship to deadlines
  • Conflict — open disagreement vs harmony preservation

Quick cultural cheat sheet

CultureNotes
American (US)Direct, low hierarchy, fast pace, friendly tone. "Yes" means yes; "we should catch up" usually means nothing. Optimistic framing expected.
British (UK)Indirect via politeness. "Quite good" = "pretty bad." "Interesting" can be a polite skepticism. Master understatement and self-deprecation.
German / SwissVery direct, low-context. "This is wrong" is normal, not rude. Punctuality is non-negotiable. No small talk before business.
Dutch / ScandinavianDirect, low-hierarchy, egalitarian. Disagreeing with seniors is expected. Pleasantries minimal.
FrenchHierarchical but intellectually combative. Debate is a sign of respect. Form (proper greetings, titles) matters.
JapaneseHigh-context, harmony-preserving. "Maybe difficult" = "no." Silence has meaning. Hierarchy is significant.
SingaporeanMix of British politeness, Chinese hierarchy, direct workplace norms. Generally efficient and pragmatic.
AustralianDirect, irreverent, anti-hierarchy. Joking around is normal. Excessive formality reads as cold.
IsraeliExtremely direct. Disagreement is constant and not personal. Hierarchy minimal. Don't take sharpness personally.
Latin AmericanRelationship-first; small talk before business. Warmer, more expressive. Time more flexible.

The "yes" that isn't yes

In high-context cultures (including Indian, Japanese, much of Asia), saying a direct "no" feels rude. People say:

  • "We'll see"
  • "That might be difficult"
  • "Let me think about it" (and never get back)
  • "I'll try"

To international colleagues, these read as "yes" and they expect delivery. Internally for you: say no when you mean no. "I won't be able to do that by Friday — earliest I can is Tuesday."

Working across timezones

  • Document everything. If a decision happens in your morning, the US side wakes up 8 hours later — they need to read what happened.
  • Use async-first. Default to written; meetings are the exception.
  • Overlap windows are precious. If you have 2 hours of overlap with the US, don't waste it on stand-ups — use it for synchronous decision-making.
  • Be explicit about timezones. "EOD" differs by 12+ hours. Always include the city or zone.
  • Hand-offs: if work crosses time zones, leave clear notes for the next person.

Email/Slack tone for international audiences

  • Lead with the ask, not the context. Most international cultures front-load.
  • Skip the "respected sir" greeting. First names are standard.
  • Don't apologize for things you didn't do wrong. "Sorry to bother you" is polite in Indian English; in some cultures it reads as weak.
  • Be specific about timelines. "By Friday Nov 14 at 5pm IST" beats "soon."
  • Confirm understanding. Repeat back asks: "So you'd like X by Y — is that right?"

When you don't understand the accent

Common with US Southern, Scottish, Irish, heavy Indian regional, French, etc. Tactics:

  • "Sorry, you broke up there — could you repeat that?"
  • "Just to make sure I caught that — were you saying [your interpretation]?"
  • "Could you slow down a touch? Audio is a bit fuzzy."
  • Ask in chat: "Could you type the URL/name?"
  • Watch their lips on video — helps comprehension significantly

Asking is fine. Pretending and acting on the wrong info is the worse mistake.

Being part of a "remote first" team

Common in modern tech. Norms:

  • Decisions happen in writing, in shared docs / channels — not in private DMs
  • Verbose written communication is encouraged ("over-communicate")
  • Visibility on what you're doing is your responsibility — share progress proactively
  • Camera-on for calls (default), camera-off acceptable when needed
  • Status updates async — daily or weekly written summaries replace stand-ups

The "trust gap"

Working remotely with people you've never met means earning trust without face-time. Build it through:

  • Reliability — do what you said you'd do, by when
  • Visibility — share progress, problems, questions
  • Helpfulness — answer others' questions, even outside your area
  • Consistency — same person across messages, meetings, code

"I am working in this company since 5 years.""I've been working at this company for 5 years."
"You have come from where?""Where are you from?"
"He is my college only.""He's also at my college."
"Same to same.""They're the same / identical."
"I am also having that book.""I have that book too."

Tense / aspect patterns to watch

Indian English uses progressive ("-ing") forms more broadly than other dialects. Stative verbs (know, have, see, want, like) usually don't take progressive form internationally:

Indian EnglishInternational
I am having two brothers.I have two brothers.
I am knowing him since school.I've known him since school.
She is liking the movie.She likes the movie.
I am wanting to go.I want to go.
He is seeing the doctor today.He's seeing the doctor today. (OK — "see" can be progressive when it means "meet")

Article use

Articles ("a/an/the") are tricky because Hindi doesn't have direct equivalents. Common patterns:

Indian EnglishInternational
I went to office.I went to the office.
He is going to bank.He's going to the bank.
She is working in software company.She works at a software company.
I want to watch movie.I want to watch a movie.
Give me one pen.Give me a pen.

"Itself" and "only" emphasis

Indian English uses "only" and "itself" for emphasis where international English uses different structures:

Indian EnglishInternational
I told him today only.I told him just today.
I will do it now only.I'll do it right now.
I myself will do it.I'll do it myself.
That is the place itself.That's the place.
He is from Mumbai only.He's from Mumbai.

Pronunciation patterns common in Indian English

  • Retroflex T and D instead of alveolar — Indian "tomato" vs American/British "tomato"
  • V/W merger — "very" and "wery" sound similar
  • Equal stress on syllables instead of one stressed syllable per word — this affects rhythm most.
  • Trilled R — somewhat softer R needed in international audiences
  • Z to J shift — "zoo" sounding closer to "joo"
  • "S" before consonants — "school" pronounced "ischool" — drop the leading vowel

What to keep, what to change

The goal is not to lose your Indian-English voice. Indian English is a valid, beautiful dialect spoken by hundreds of millions. The goal is bilingual fluency: speak it freely with Indian colleagues; switch to international neutral when working internationally.

Things to consciously change for international contexts:

  • The unique vocabulary above (use international equivalents)
  • Stress patterns (one stressed syllable per word)
  • Article use (where it differs)
  • Stative verb tense (don't say "I am having")

Things to keep without apology:

  • Your accent — most international colleagues understand Indian accents fine
  • Polite, relationship-aware tone — it's an asset
  • Indirect framing in difficult conversations — softer can be useful
  • Detail orientation in writing

Glossary

Common terms used throughout this course, in plain English.

Active listening
Listening fully, without planning your reply, then confirming you understood before responding.
Active voice
Subject-verb-object: "The team shipped the feature." Direct, clear. (Compare: passive voice — "The feature was shipped by the team.")
Anchor
The first number or position in a negotiation. Subsequent discussion centers around it.
Async / asynchronous
Communication where the sender and receiver aren't online at the same time — email, Slack messages, doc comments.
BATNA
Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement. What you'll do if the negotiation fails. Knowing your BATNA gives you walking power.
Body language
Physical communication: posture, gestures, facial expressions, eye contact. Often louder than your words.
Diplomatic language
Soft framing of hard messages. Used to deliver difficult content without provoking defensiveness.
Disagree and commit
You voice your disagreement, the decision goes against you, you commit to executing it as if it were yours. Standard in modern tech culture.
EOD
End of day. Always specify timezone — "EOD IST" or "EOD PT," not just "EOD."
FAQ
Frequently Asked Questions. A doc section answering predictable questions.
Filler words
"Um," "uh," "like," "basically," "you know" — sounds we make while thinking. Replace with silence.
Front-load
Put the most important info at the start (subject line, first sentence, first slide). The opposite of burying.
Hedge words
"Maybe," "might," "could," "perhaps." Used to soften statements. Can also weaken when overused.
High-context vs low-context
High-context cultures (India, Japan) imply much; low-context (Germany, US) state explicitly.
Idiom
A phrase whose meaning isn't literal: "ballpark figure," "kick the can down the road."
Intonation
The rise and fall of pitch in speech. Affects whether something sounds like a question, statement, or sarcasm.
Modal verb
Helper verbs: can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must. Communicate certainty, permission, ability.
Phrasal verb
Verb + particle that changes the meaning: "look up" (search), "look after" (care for), "look into" (investigate).
PREP framework
Point-Reason-Example-Point. A structure for impromptu speaking.
Q&A
Questions and answers — usually the section after a presentation.
Recap
Brief summary at the end of a meeting / email confirming decisions and next steps.
RFC
Request For Comments. A long-form proposal document for a design or strategy decision.
SBI framework
Situation-Behavior-Impact. Structure for giving feedback factually.
Schwa
The "uh" sound that unstressed syllables collapse to in English: the "a" in "about," the "e" in "taken."
Small talk
Light, polite conversation about non-substantive topics (weather, weekend plans). Builds rapport.
STAR framework
Situation-Task-Action-Result. Structure for behavioral interview answers.
Stress (in pronunciation)
The emphasis given to certain syllables. English is stress-timed: stressed syllables are loud and long, unstressed are short and quiet.
Stress-timed vs syllable-timed
English is stress-timed (rhythm based on stresses). Many Indian languages are syllable-timed (each syllable similar length). The difference is the most audible accent feature.
Sync (synchronous)
Real-time communication — meeting, call, in-person conversation. Opposite of async.
TL;DR
"Too Long; Didn't Read." A 1-3 sentence summary at the top of a long doc or message.
Tone
The emotional flavor of communication: casual, formal, friendly, hostile, neutral.
Up-talk
Ending statements with rising intonation, making them sound like questions. Reads as uncertain.

90-Day Practice Plan

Communication is a practice skill. You can read this whole course and improve nothing if you don't act on it. Here's a 90-day plan: 15-30 minutes a day, mostly built into work you're already doing.

Days 1-30: Foundations

WeekFocusDaily action
Week 1 Listening In every meeting, paraphrase someone's point at least once. ("So if I'm hearing right, you're saying X — is that fair?")
Week 2 Speaking up In every meeting you attend, contribute at least one substantive comment (not just "+1"). Track in a notebook.
Week 3 Pronunciation Pick 5 problem words a day (where you know your accent diverges). Practice them 10 times each. Use Forvo or YouGlish.
Week 4 Email discipline Every email: subject line specific, ask in first sentence, under 200 words. Review your sent folder Friday.

Days 31-60: Building skills

WeekFocusDaily action
Week 5 Killing fillers Record one short voice memo a day. Listen back. Count "um" / "uh." Goal: zero by end of week.
Week 6 Stand-ups 60-second update max. Practice the night before. Eliminate vague phrases like "worked on the project."
Week 7 Disagreeing well Pick one moment a day to push back politely. Use the validate-pivot-position-open framework.
Week 8 Saying no For every new request, pause before saying yes. Ask the trade-off question: "If I take this on, what should slip?"

Days 61-90: Putting it together

WeekFocusDaily action
Week 9 Writing Write or update one piece of documentation each day. Apply: TL;DR, headers, short sentences.
Week 10 Presentations Volunteer to present in some forum (lunch-and-learn, demo, RFC). Prepare with structure + rehearsal.
Week 11 Networking Reach out to 5 people (cold or warm) with specific, low-ask messages. Write back to 3 thank-yous.
Week 12 Hard conversations Have one conversation you've been avoiding. Use SBI framework. Send written follow-up.

Daily micro-habits

  • 📖 Read 10 minutes of native English a day (book, blog, news). Notice patterns.
  • 🎧 Listen to a podcast on your commute. Repeat one sentence aloud, copying their rhythm.
  • ✍️ Journal 3 sentences about your day in English. Builds fluency over months.
  • 🗣️ Speak to yourself in English while doing chores or commuting. Sounds silly; works.
  • 📝 Re-read one of your sent emails at the end of the day. What would you cut?

Get a feedback partner

Find one person — colleague, friend, mentor — who'll honestly tell you what to fix. Once a month, spend 30 minutes with them. Show them an email you sent, ask "what would land better?" Watch a recording of you presenting, ask for one specific improvement.

Solo practice plateaus around month 3. External feedback unsticks you.

The Fluency Mindset

You've reached the end of a long course. Before you close it, the most important section: the mindset that determines whether any of it actually changes you.

Fluency is not perfection

"Fluent" doesn't mean you sound like a native speaker. It doesn't mean zero mistakes. Fluent means: you express what you mean, and the listener understands it, comfortably. Native speakers stumble, restart, mispronounce, search for words. Fluency is comfort under uncertainty, not a flawless tape recording.

The accent is fine

Most international colleagues understand Indian accents fine — better than they understand Scottish, Australian regional, or Southern US accents. If you can be understood, the accent is doing its job. Don't waste mental energy trying to erase it.

What does matter:

  • Stress patterns (one stressed syllable per word)
  • Pace (slow enough to be heard)
  • Volume and projection
  • Clarity of consonants in critical words

The "good English" myth

There's no single "correct" English. There's American English, British English, Indian English, Australian English, Singaporean English — all valid, all spoken by hundreds of millions. The skill is not "speaking correct English"; it's code-switching: knowing which version is appropriate for which context, and switching between them.

You speak Indian English with your team in Bangalore; international neutral English with your colleagues in Berlin and Boston. Both are you.

Confidence beats vocabulary

A hesitant speaker with a 10,000-word vocabulary loses the room to a confident speaker with a 3,000-word vocabulary. People listen to people who sound like they mean what they say. Confidence comes from:

  • Preparation (you know what you want to say)
  • Acceptance (you're OK with not being perfect)
  • Repetition (you've done this enough times that it doesn't scare you)

Speak before you're ready

The trap most learners fall into: waiting to be "good enough" before speaking up in real situations. The truth: you only get good through real situations. The lunch-and-learn talk, the meeting comment, the cold-outreach email — they're not graduation tests. They're the practice itself.

Volunteer for the talk before you feel ready. Send the email before you've polished it five times. Speak up in the meeting before you've finalized your opinion. The mistakes won't kill you. The silence will.

Translate less, think more

Real fluency comes when you stop translating from Hindi (or your first language) into English and start thinking in English. The path:

  1. Translate every sentence in your head (slow, awkward)
  2. Translate phrases (faster)
  3. Speak directly without translation for common situations (fluid)
  4. Think in English when discussing English-context topics (full fluency)

This shifts naturally with exposure. Watch English shows without subtitles. Read English books. Have full conversations in English. The brain adapts.

Mistakes are data, not failure

Every "wrong" word, every awkward phrase, every misunderstanding is information about where to focus next. Treat them like compiler errors: useful, specific, fixable. Not like personal flaws.

Native speakers make mistakes too — they just don't apologize for them. Adopt the same posture.

The 1% rule

Improve 1% a week. Over a year, that's a 67% improvement. Over three years, you're a fundamentally different communicator. The trap is wanting fluency in 3 months and giving up when you're not there. The reality is fluency takes 3 years of consistent practice — and gets there reliably for those who keep going.

Some things you can't fake

  • Curiosity. If you're genuinely interested in what others say, listening becomes effortless.
  • Honesty. "I don't know" beats fluent bluffing every time.
  • Care. The audience can tell when you actually care about helping them understand vs performing.
  • Effort. Show up prepared. Trumps natural talent.

The compounding effect

Each communication skill compounds with the others. Better listening makes you a better speaker (you understand audiences). Better writing makes you a better thinker (writing forces clarity). Better presentations make you a better one-on-one communicator (you've learned to structure your thinking).

Don't try to fix everything at once. Pick one area, work on it for a month, then move on. By year three, every dimension has improved.

The final thought

Communication is the bridge between what you know and what the world receives from you. You can be the most brilliant engineer in the building, and if you can't share your thinking, your impact is limited to what you can build alone. The opposite is also true: a fairly good engineer who communicates well can lead teams, shape strategy, and outperform their pure technical peers.

The work of becoming a great communicator is one of the highest-leverage things you can do for your career. It's also one of the most generous — clear communication is a gift you give to everyone you work with.

Start small. Speak up in tomorrow's meeting. Write a clean email. Pause before saying yes. The skill builds, conversation by conversation, year by year.

Good luck. You've got this.
